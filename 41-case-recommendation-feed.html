Case Study: Recommendation Feed

Design the “For You” feed for a short-video app: 400 million daily users, a catalogue of 2 billion videos, a new video must be able to find its audience within minutes, and the whole thing has to render in under 200 ms.

  • Turn a vague product brief into a metric and a budget
  • Design the retrieval → ranking → re-ranking pipeline at this scale
  • Handle freshness, cold start and the feedback loop
  • Size the serving fleet and the near-line pipelines
  • Say what you'd ship first and what breaks at 10×

1 · Requirements

DAU400M
Feed requests/s (peak)350k
Catalogue2B videos
New uploads/day80M
Latency budget200 ms p95
Items per response10

In scope: ranking the personalised feed. Out of scope: video encoding and delivery, creator tools, ads insertion (a separate auction that interleaves with these results), moderation (Chapter 51).

🎯
Metrics, stated before any architecture

Primary (online): next-day retention, plus daily time-spent-per-user as a secondary. Ranking proxies (offline): multi-task AUC for watch-completion, like, share and follow; NDCG@10.
Guardrails: p95 latency, creator concentration (share of impressions to the top 1% of creators), new-video exposure rate, reported-content rate, and the share of sessions ending in an explicit “not interested”.
Baseline: trending-by-region plus content from accounts the user follows. It's a genuinely strong baseline, and it's what runs when the model fails.

2 · Architecture

One feed requestStep-through
  1. User id, device, locale, time, session state (what they've just watched and for how long). Session context is the strongest short-term signal you have — a user who watched three cooking videos in a row is telling you more than their entire history.
  2. Two-tower ANN retrieval on the user embedding (~1,500), co-visitation from the last watched videos (~800), followed creators (~400), trending in region (~600), fresh uploads under 6 hours old (~500), and a bandit-driven exploration slate (~200). Each source has its own budget and its own recall metric.
  3. Remove already-seen (a Bloom filter per user keeps this cheap), blocked creators, muted topics, region-restricted and policy-flagged content. Roughly 4,000 → 3,200.
  4. A deliberately cheap model — a few dozen features, no cross-attention — scores everything and keeps the top ~500. This stage exists purely so the expensive ranker sees a tractable number of items, and its own quality metric is “does the item the heavy ranker would have chosen survive?”.
  5. A multi-task network predicts P(watch ≥ 80%), P(like), P(share), P(follow), P(hide). Hundreds of features: user history embeddings, creator stats, video content embeddings, crosses. One forward pass over 500 items, batched on GPU, ~25 ms.
  6. Combine heads into one score with product-negotiated weights, then apply constraints: at most 2 per creator in 10, topic diversity, a minimum share of fresh and exploratory items, and ad slots. Return 10.
  7. Every item shown is logged with its position and the scores that produced it. Without impressions you cannot compute propensities, build negatives or evaluate off-policy — this single decision determines whether your training data is usable (Chapter 30).
  8. Engagement events stream back within seconds. Video-level statistics update continuously; user embeddings refresh every few minutes; the ranker is retrained hourly on a sliding window and the retrieval towers nightly. Freshness of statistics matters more than freshness of weights.
  9. New models flow into serving through the standard shadow → canary → A/B path (Chapter 33), with the guardrails from the metrics section attached as automatic rollback conditions.

3 · Deep dive: the latency budget

⏱️
Every stage needs its own timeout and its own fallback

At 350k requests per second, something is always slow. Each candidate source has a deadline and is simply omitted if it misses; if the heavy ranker is unavailable, the pre-rank ordering is served; if everything fails, the trending baseline is served. The feed must never fail to render. Each degradation is logged so you can see the quality cost of a bad minute.

5 · Deep dive: cold start

New video (80M/day)

No engagement history, so collaborative signals are empty. It enters via the fresh and exploration slots, scored from content embeddings (video, audio, caption, OCR), creator priors and early-viewer response. The first few hundred impressions are effectively a measurement experiment: a bandit allocates them and the video graduates to normal retrieval within minutes if the early signal is strong. Guaranteeing that exposure budget is what keeps the catalogue alive.

New user

Region and device popularity for the first few items, updated after every interaction — the first session is where retention is won or lost, so the user embedding is recomputed within the session rather than nightly. An optional interest-picker at signup helps, but observed behaviour overtakes stated preference within about twenty videos.

6 · Deep dive: the feedback loop

🌀
The failure mode that defines this product

The model shows what it predicts you'll watch → you watch it → that becomes training data → it becomes more certain. Within weeks, catalogue coverage narrows, a few creators take most impressions, and the system is blind to everything it stopped showing. Structural defences, all of which cost short-term engagement: a guaranteed exploration budget, impression logging with propensities for off-policy evaluation, diversity caps in the mixer, a floor on new-creator exposure, and monitoring coverage and concentration as SLOs rather than as curiosities. Report them next to engagement in every launch review, or they will lose every argument.

8 · What I'd ship first, and what breaks at 10×

🚀 Week one

Trending + followed baseline, full impression logging, and the metric dashboard. You cannot train anything useful without logged impressions, and the baseline is what the model must beat. Then: co-visitation candidates and a simple GBDT ranker. Two-tower retrieval and multi-task deep ranking come after there's data and a measurement loop.

💥 At 10×

The heavy ranker's GPU cost becomes the binding constraint — the fix is a better pre-ranker, not a bigger cluster. The seen-items filter grows unboundedly (bound it by time). The event pipeline's impression volume outgrows the warehouse budget, so you sample impressions while keeping all engagements. And feedback-loop effects that were tolerable become structural, because the model's influence on the catalogue grows with its reach.

🎤
Interview drill

Follow-up: “Engagement is up 6% but creators are complaining.” What do you look at?

How a strong answer sounds
Creator-side metrics, which the engagement number deliberately hides. Concentration: what share of impressions goes to the top 1% of creators, and how has it moved? New-creator exposure: is a first upload still getting its measurement budget? Catalogue coverage: how many distinct videos received meaningful impressions this week versus last? If concentration rose, the model is exploiting a narrowing set of reliable winners, which raises short-term engagement and starves supply — and in a two-sided market, supply leaving is a much slower and much more expensive problem than a 6% engagement dip. I'd check whether the exploration budget was reduced or the diversity caps loosened in the same release, and I'd put those guardrails into the launch criteria so this trade is made deliberately rather than discovered.

Follow-up: “Why not skip retrieval and rank everything with the big model?”

How a strong answer sounds
The arithmetic: 2 billion items × 350,000 requests per second is 7×10¹⁴ scorings per second. Even at a microsecond each that's impossible by many orders of magnitude, and the heavy ranker takes far longer than a microsecond per item. The funnel exists so each stage spends the most compute it can afford on the number of items it sees — and the metric that links the stages is candidate recall, because the ranker can only order what retrieval returned. The interesting engineering question isn't whether to have a funnel, it's how to measure what each stage loses.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Define retention as the primary metric and engagement proxies as ranking targets — and guardrail the ecosystem.
  • Six candidate sources with independent budgets, timeouts and recall metrics beat one clever retriever.
  • A cheap pre-ranker is what makes the expensive ranker affordable; measure what it discards.
  • Log impressions with positions and scores, or your training data and off-policy evaluation are unusable.
  • Exploration budgets and diversity caps are the structural defence against the feedback loop.

Check your understanding

Why log impressions and not just engagements?

Without knowing what was shown, you can't construct negatives, correct for position bias, or estimate how an alternative ranker would have performed.

A candidate source is slow at p99. What should the system do?

Each source has its own deadline. Losing 600 candidates costs a little quality; missing the latency budget costs the whole request. Log the drop so the quality cost is visible.

How does a brand-new video get its first audience?

Waiting for engagement is circular. A reserved exploration budget buys the measurement, and strong early signal graduates the video into normal retrieval within minutes.

Which is the clearest sign the feedback loop is closing?

The system converges on a narrowing set of reliable winners. Engagement can rise while the catalogue quietly dies, which is why coverage and concentration belong in the launch criteria.

What is the pre-ranker's own success metric?

It exists to shrink the candidate set without discarding what matters. Agreement with the heavy ranker's top items — recall of the eventual winners — is the property to measure.
