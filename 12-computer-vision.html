An image is a tensor

A 224×224 RGB photo is a tensor of shape (3, 224, 224) — three channel planes of 8-bit intensities, normalised to roughly zero mean and unit variance per channel before it reaches the network. A batch adds a dimension: (B, 3, 224, 224). Everything else is arithmetic on that block.

🎨
Match the preprocessing to the pretrained model

If you fine-tune a model trained with ImageNet normalization, use those channel means and standard deviations, that resize strategy and that channel order (RGB vs BGR). A silent mismatch here costs several accuracy points and produces no error message — it's one of the most common transfer-learning bugs.

Convolution, concretely

Slide a small grid of weights (the kernel) across the image; at each position compute a dot product between the kernel and the patch underneath. One kernel detects one kind of local pattern. A conv layer has hundreds of them, and the network learns what they should be.

Why convolution instead of a dense layer?

A dense layer connecting 224×224×3 inputs to 1,000 units has 150 million weights, and it learns “an eye in the top-left” separately from “an eye in the middle”. A convolution applies the same small kernel everywhere: translation equivariance for free, thousands of times fewer parameters, and a strong prior that nearby pixels matter together. That inductive bias is why CNNs worked when data was scarce.

Output size = ⌊(in + 2·pad − kernel)/stride⌋ + 1. “Same” padding means pad = (kernel−1)/2 with stride 1, which keeps the size unchanged.

The architecture lineage

Each generation fixed a specific problem. Knowing which is far more useful than memorising layer counts.

LeNet (1998) · AlexNet (2012)

Convolution + pooling + ReLU on GPUs, with dropout and augmentation. AlexNet's ImageNet win is the moment deep learning became the default.

VGG (2014)

Showed that stacks of small 3×3 kernels beat a few large ones: same receptive field, fewer parameters, more nonlinearity. Very deep, very heavy.

ResNet (2015)

Residual connections. Suddenly 50–150 layers trained reliably, because gradients have an identity path home. Still the default backbone in production a decade later.

MobileNet / EfficientNet (2017–19)

Depthwise-separable convolutions and compound scaling: accuracy per FLOP became the objective, making on-device vision practical.

Vision Transformer (2020)

Cut the image into 16×16 patches and treat them as tokens (Chapter 14). Weak spatial prior, so it needs more data or strong augmentation — but it scales better and unifies vision with language modelling.

ConvNeXt, hybrids, SAM, CLIP (2022–)

Modernised CNNs match ViTs; contrastive image–text training (CLIP) gives open-vocabulary recognition; promptable segmentation and vision-language models turn perception into a general interface.

🎁
Start from pretrained weights. Always.

For nearly every applied vision task, fine-tuning a pretrained backbone on a few thousand labelled images beats training from scratch on a hundred thousand. Freeze early layers if data is very small, fine-tune everything with a low learning rate if you have more, and use a discriminative learning rate (lower for early layers) if you want the last 1%.

The four task shapes

Classification

One label per image. Metric: accuracy, top-5, macro-F1.

Detection

Boxes + labels. Metric: mAP over IoU thresholds. Needs NMS.

Segmentation

A label per pixel. Metric: mIoU, Dice. Semantic vs instance vs panoptic.

Keypoints / pose

Coordinates per joint. Metric: PCK, OKS. Feeds tracking and AR.

Detection mechanics: IoU and NMS

Detectors propose far more boxes than there are objects. IoU (intersection over union) measures box overlap; non-maximum suppression keeps the highest-scoring box and deletes others that overlap it beyond a threshold.

The systems half of vision

At 224×224 a ResNet-50 forward pass is a few milliseconds on a modern GPU. Decoding a 4 MB JPEG and running five augmentations takes tens of milliseconds of CPU. Without enough workers, GPU-side decoding, pre-resized shards or a sequential file format, your expensive accelerator waits on libjpeg. Always profile the loader before profiling the model (Chapter 11).

Deploying vision models

☁️ Server-side

Batch requests, use fp16/int8, export to a runtime like TensorRT or ONNX Runtime. Resolution is your biggest cost lever: cost scales with pixels, and 320² is often within a point of 640².

📱 On-device

Quantized MobileNet/EfficientNet-class models, NPU delegates, and a strict memory budget. Privacy and latency wins; update cadence becomes an app-release problem.

🎥 Streaming video

Don't run the model on every frame: detect every k frames and track in between, or use motion gating. This is a 10× cost reduction that no model change can match (Chapter 50).

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Augmenting the validation set. Evaluation must be deterministic — resize and centre-crop only.
  • Different preprocessing in training and serving. A different resize interpolation or normalization constant quietly costs accuracy points.
  • Flipping images where orientation matters. Left/right matters in medical imaging, driving and any task involving text.
  • Ignoring class imbalance in detection. Background boxes outnumber objects thousands to one; that's why focal loss and hard-negative mining exist.
  • Training at a resolution you can't afford to serve. Decide the inference resolution first, then train for it.
  • Leaking near-duplicate frames across splits. Consecutive video frames are almost identical — split by clip or by scene, never by frame.

Interview drill

Warm-up: Why do two stacked 3×3 convolutions often beat one 5×5?

How a strong answer sounds
They have the same 5×5 receptive field but fewer parameters (2·9·C² vs 25·C²) and include an extra nonlinearity, so the composite function is more expressive per parameter. They're also better supported by hardware kernels. That observation — from VGG — is why nearly every CNN since uses small kernels stacked deep rather than large kernels stacked shallow.

Deeper: Your defect detector gets 0.91 mAP offline but misses obvious defects in the factory. What do you check?

How a strong answer sounds
First, distribution shift: the test set is probably from the same capture session as training — different line, lighting, camera or lens in production. I'd collect a few hundred production images, label them, and evaluate on that. Second, preprocessing parity: resize, normalization and colour space must match exactly between the training pipeline and the edge runtime; quantization can also shift behaviour. Third, the operating point: mAP averages over thresholds, but the line runs at one confidence threshold, so I'd measure recall at the deployed threshold on the specific defect classes that matter. The fix is usually a data engine — collect production failures continuously, label them, retrain — rather than a better architecture.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Convolution shares one small kernel across the image: translation equivariance and far fewer parameters.
  • Stride, padding and depth determine output size and receptive field — compute them, don't guess.
  • ResNet's skip connections made depth trainable; ViTs trade spatial priors for scale.
  • Detection needs IoU, NMS and mAP; segmentation needs per-pixel metrics like mIoU.
  • Augmentation is the cheapest regularizer, and the data pipeline is usually the real bottleneck.

Check your understanding

Input 64×64, kernel 3, stride 2, padding 1. What is the output size?

⌊(64 + 2 − 3)/2⌋ + 1 = ⌊31.5⌋ + 1 = 32. Stride-2 with “same” padding halves each spatial dimension — the standard downsampling step.

What problem do residual connections solve?

The identity path has derivative 1, so gradients reach early layers undiminished. That's what made 50–1,000-layer networks trainable.

You have 4,000 labelled images of a specialised industrial part. Best approach?

Pretrained features transfer remarkably well. Training a data-hungry architecture from scratch on 4,000 images will badly underperform a fine-tuned ResNet or ConvNeXt.

Why does a detector need non-maximum suppression?

Dense predictions produce clusters of similar boxes. NMS keeps the highest-scoring one per cluster using an IoU threshold — too high and you get duplicates, too low and you delete genuinely adjacent objects.

Your GPU sits at 35% utilization while training an image classifier. Most likely fix?

JPEG decode plus CPU-side augmentation usually can't keep up. More augmentation would make it worse; the answer is a faster pipeline or GPU-side decoding.
