Case Study: Ad Click Prediction

Predict the click-through rate of an ad, one million times a second, in under ten milliseconds, well enough that an auction can charge real money against it. This is the most latency-constrained, most calibration-critical, most adversarially-incentivised ML system in common use.

  • Understand why calibration — not ranking — is the requirement here
  • Handle billions of sparse feature values in a fixed memory budget
  • Meet a 10 ms budget at 1M QPS
  • Train with delayed conversions and position bias
  • Handle budget pacing, exploration and cold-start advertisers

💷
This is a pricing model, not a ranking model

The auction computes eCPM = bid × P(click) (and for conversion campaigns, × P(convert | click)). The prediction directly sets what an advertiser pays. A model with perfect ranking but systematically inflated probabilities overcharges everyone and quietly destroys advertiser trust; deflated probabilities under-deliver and leave revenue on the table. Calibration is the product requirement — ranking quality alone is not sufficient (Chapter 7).

🎯
Metrics

Offline: log loss (proper scoring rule — rewards honest probabilities), calibration error overall and per slice (advertiser, placement, device, country), and AUC as a secondary. Online: revenue per thousand requests, advertiser ROI/ROAS, fill rate, and delivery against budget. Guardrails: p99 latency, per-advertiser over/under-delivery, and new-advertiser exposure. Baseline: historical CTR per (ad, placement) with Bayesian shrinkage — genuinely strong for head advertisers, useless for new ones.

🗜️
Hashing plus embeddings, with a bounded table

Cross features are effectively unbounded, so hash them into a fixed slot space (Chapter 4) — collisions between two rare values cost little. Head ids get learned embeddings; the tail shares hashed buckets and leans on descriptive features. The embedding table is the model: tens of gigabytes, sharded across parameter servers, with frequency-based admission (an id must be seen N times before it gets its own row) and eviction of ids that stop appearing. Fixed memory is a hard design constraint, not an optimisation.

🖥️
This runs on CPUs, and that's deliberate

The model is shallow and the work is dominated by sparse lookups rather than dense matrix multiplication, so GPUs offer little — and CPU fleets co-located with the ad server avoid a network hop that would consume a quarter of the budget. The scaling lever is horizontal: thousands of small replicas, each holding a shard of the embedding table or reading from a co-located store. Serving cost is measured in fractions of a cent per thousand requests, and at 500M scorings a second, a 5% efficiency gain is a large number.

5 · Deep dive: delayed conversions

A click is observed in seconds. A conversion may arrive 30 days later — or never. Training a conversion model on “has it converted yet?” systematically labels recent positives as negatives.

⏳

Wait for the window

Correct labels, but the model is always 30 days stale. Unusable alone in a market that moves weekly.

📐

Delay modelling

Model the conversion-delay distribution explicitly and correct the likelihood for censored examples. The standard approach — it lets you train on recent data honestly.

🔁

Importance weighting

Train on fresh data with weights reflecting the probability that a conversion has not yet landed, and re-ingest positives as they arrive.

🥶
Cold-start advertisers and the exploration tax

A new campaign has no impressions, so its predicted CTR is the prior, so it loses auctions, so it never gets impressions. Left alone, the marketplace ossifies around incumbents. Fixes: optimistic priors from similar creatives and advertisers (a hierarchical model that borrows strength from the campaign, advertiser and category levels), an explicit exploration budget — Thompson sampling over the CTR posterior — and a guaranteed minimum impression allocation for new campaigns. This costs measurable short-term revenue and is the price of a functioning market, exactly as in Chapter 41.

🎤
Interview drill

Follow-up: “AUC improved 1.2% and revenue fell.” How?

How a strong answer sounds
AUC only measures ordering; the auction consumes the probability. If the new model ranks slightly better but is systematically under-confident, every eCPM drops, fewer auctions clear the reserve price, and revenue falls despite better ranking. Or the miscalibration is uneven — better for large advertisers, worse for a segment — which shifts who wins and at what price. I'd check calibration overall and per slice first, refit the calibration map, and re-run. It's also why log loss rather than AUC should be the headline offline metric here: it's a proper scoring rule, so it can't be improved by distorting probabilities.

Follow-up: “Third-party cookies are going away.” What changes?

How a strong answer sounds
The user-identity features — cross-site history and long-lived segments — get much weaker or disappear, which hits the features that carried most of the personalisation signal. The system shifts weight toward first-party and contextual signals: page and creative content embeddings, placement, session behaviour within the site, and coarse cohort-level features. Conversion measurement moves to aggregated and privacy-preserving APIs, so attribution becomes noisier and delayed — which means the conversion model needs delay modelling and noise-aware training rather than exact per-user joins. Practically I'd expect a real accuracy drop, so the honest plan is to re-baseline expectations, invest in contextual and creative understanding, and rebuild evaluation around aggregate measurement rather than pretending the old per-user pipeline still works.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • The probability is a price: calibration, measured per slice, is the primary requirement.
  • Hashing plus frequency-gated embeddings keeps an unbounded feature space in fixed memory.
  • A 10 ms budget at 500M scorings/second means shallow models on co-located CPUs.
  • Delayed conversions need explicit delay modelling, not “has it converted yet?” labels.
  • Pacing and cold-start exploration are market-health requirements that cost short-term revenue.

Check your understanding

Why is calibration more important than AUC in ad CTR prediction?

eCPM = bid × P(click). A systematically inflated probability overcharges advertisers; a deflated one leaves revenue unearned. Ranking alone doesn't determine either.

You downsample negatives 10:1 for training. What must you do?

The model now believes positives are ten times more common than they are. Without correction every predicted probability — and therefore every price — is inflated.

A conversion can arrive up to 30 days after the click. What's the standard training approach?

Waiting makes the model 30 days stale; naive negatives systematically mislabel recent positives. Delay modelling lets you train on fresh data honestly.

Why do these systems typically serve on CPUs rather than GPUs?

There's little dense matrix work to accelerate, and the round trip to a separate GPU tier would consume much of the latency budget.

A new advertiser's campaign gets no impressions. Why, and what's the fix?

A self-reinforcing cold start. Borrow strength from advertiser and category levels, and reserve exploration impressions so new campaigns can be measured at all.
