Alignment: RLHF, DPO & Reward Models

A pretrained model predicts plausible continuations of the internet. An assistant answers your question, refuses the dangerous request and admits when it doesn't know. Turning the first into the second is alignment — and it is mostly a data and evaluation problem wearing a reinforcement-learning coat.

  • Explain the SFT → reward model → RL pipeline and what each stage adds
  • Collect preference data that produces a usable reward model
  • Describe the KL penalty and what happens without it
  • Compare PPO with DPO and know when each is appropriate
  • Recognise reward hacking, sycophancy and over-refusal

The gap alignment closes

Ask a base model “How do I reset my password?” and a very plausible continuation is three more questions in the same style — because that's what web pages look like. It isn't broken; it's doing exactly what it was trained to do. Alignment changes the objective from “what text usually follows?” to “what response would a person prefer?”.

The classic three-stage pipeline

From base model to aligned assistantStep-through
  1. Pretrained on trillions of tokens (Chapter 16). It has the knowledge and the language ability; what it lacks is the conversational contract.
  2. Train on tens of thousands of high-quality demonstrations: a prompt and the response a good assistant would give (Chapter 17). This alone gets you most of the way to something usable — and it is the stage with the best effort-to-quality ratio.
  3. Sample several responses per prompt from the SFT model and have humans rank them pairwise. Comparisons are far more reliable than absolute scores — people agree on “B is better” far more than on “that's a 7/10”.
  4. A model (often the SFT model with a scalar head) learns to output a score such that preferred responses score higher. Loss: −log σ(r_chosen − r_rejected) — the Bradley–Terry model, the same maths as Elo ratings.
  5. Now use RL (PPO, or simpler variants like GRPO) to update the policy so its responses score higher under the reward model. Each step: sample responses, score them, push probability toward high-scoring ones.
  6. Subtract a KL penalty for drifting from the SFT reference policy. Without it the policy finds degenerate text that the reward model loves and humans hate — the reward model is only accurate near the distribution it was trained on.
  7. Measure win rate against the previous model with human or model judges, red-team it, check refusal calibration, then collect fresh preferences on the new model's outputs and go round again. Alignment is iterative, not a one-off stage.

Your annotators define the model's values, literally

The reward model learns whatever your labellers prefer, including biases they don't know they have: longer answers, confident tone, bullet points, American spelling. Measure inter-annotator agreement, write guidelines that state trade-offs explicitly (“prefer correct and short over plausible and long”), and audit for length bias — it's the most common and most damaging.

DPO: skip the reward model

Direct Preference Optimization observes that the optimal RLHF policy has a closed-form relationship to the reward, so you can optimise the preference data directly with a simple classification-style loss — no separate reward model, no sampling loop, no RL machinery.

L_DPO = −log σ( β·[ log π(y_w|x) − log π_ref(y_w|x)
                   − log π(y_l|x) + log π_ref(y_l|x) ] )
# y_w = preferred, y_l = rejected; π_ref frozen. Two forward passes, ordinary backprop.

The practical recipe in 2026

SFT on a few thousand excellent demonstrations → DPO (or ORPO/KTO if you only have thumbs-up/down rather than pairs) on preference data → iterate by sampling from the new model and collecting fresh preferences. Reserve full PPO-style RL for when you have a reward signal worth exploring against — verifiable rewards like unit tests passing, or a maths checker — which is also where reinforcement learning has recently made the biggest gains.

Scaling preferences: RLAIF and constitutions

Human comparisons cost £1–5 each and take days. Two standard multipliers:

🤖 RLAIF

Use a strong model as the judge to produce preference labels. Far cheaper and surprisingly competitive — but it inherits the judge's biases, so keep a human-labelled subset to measure agreement and catch drift.

📜 Constitutional AI

Give the model a written set of principles; have it critique and revise its own responses against them, then train on the revisions. Makes the value judgements explicit, auditable and editable — a document rather than a labelling instruction.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Optimising the reward model score as the goal. It's a proxy that stops being valid exactly where you're pushing it hardest.
  • Collecting preferences on an old model's outputs. The reward model must see on-policy samples or it scores a distribution that no longer exists.
  • Not measuring false refusals. Over-refusal is as much a product failure as unsafe output, and far easier to ship accidentally.
  • Skipping SFT. RL on top of a base model is dramatically harder; demonstrations get you 80% of the way for a fraction of the effort.
  • One-shot alignment. Preferences, jailbreaks and product needs move; this is a loop with a cadence, not a launch step.

Interview drill

Warm-up: Why does RLHF need a KL penalty against the reference policy?

How a strong answer sounds
The reward model is a learned approximation trained on samples from roughly the SFT distribution. Optimise against it without constraint and the policy drifts into regions where the reward model has never seen data and is simply wrong — producing text that scores brilliantly and reads terribly. The KL term keeps the policy near the distribution where the proxy is trustworthy. It's the standard answer to Goodhart's law: constrain the optimiser rather than trust the measure.

Deeper: Your aligned model is measurably more helpful but 30% worse at code. What do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
That's the alignment tax, usually from a preference set dominated by conversational prompts, so gradients pull the model toward chatty behaviour and away from precise code. Diagnose first: is it formatting (wrapping code in prose, refusing to output long files) or genuine capability loss? Formatting is fixable with targeted preference data. For real regression: mix code demonstrations and code preference pairs into the tuning set, lower the learning rate and epochs, tighten the KL if it's an RL stage, and consider tuning a separate adapter for code paths. Above all, put code benchmarks into the standard eval suite so every alignment run reports them — the tax is only invisible if you don't measure it.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • SFT teaches the contract; preference optimisation refines the ranking among plausible answers.
  • A reward model is trained on pairwise comparisons with a Bradley–Terry loss — the same maths as Elo.
  • The KL penalty keeps the policy where the reward model is still accurate; without it you get reward hacking.
  • DPO achieves most of RLHF's benefit with supervised-style training and far fewer moving parts.
  • Watch length bias, sycophancy, over-refusal and capability regression — and measure each explicitly.

Check your understanding

Why is preference data collected as pairwise comparisons rather than 1–10 ratings?

Absolute scales drift between annotators and across sessions. Pairwise judgements are more reliable, and Bradley–Terry converts them into a consistent scalar reward.

Removing the KL penalty from RLHF typically causes what?

The policy drifts off the distribution the reward model was trained on, where its scores are meaningless. Degenerate, repetitive text that maxes the proxy is the classic symptom.

What is DPO's main practical advantage over PPO-based RLHF?

DPO optimises preferences directly with a supervised-style loss against a frozen reference — far simpler and more stable, at the cost of not exploring new responses online.

Your aligned model refuses “how do I kill a process on Linux?”. What's the fix?

The model has learned a surface pattern rather than the concept. More safety data makes it worse; matched benign examples teach the distinction, and a measured false-refusal rate stops it regressing again.

Preferences were collected on last quarter's model. Why is that a problem now?

Reward models are only reliable near the distribution they were trained on. Off-policy data leaves them miscalibrated exactly where the current policy lives — hence the sample → label → retrain loop.
