Fine-Tuning & PEFT

Fine-tuning teaches a model behaviour — a format, a voice, a task convention. It is remarkably bad at teaching it facts, which is what people usually reach for it to do. This chapter covers when to fine-tune, how LoRA makes it cheap, and how to serve a hundred adapters on one GPU.

  • Decide between prompting, retrieval and fine-tuning — with reasons
  • Explain LoRA's low-rank update and size an adapter
  • Build an instruction-tuning dataset that doesn't wreck the base model
  • Recognise and avoid catastrophic forgetting
  • Serve many adapters efficiently, and know when to merge instead

First: should you fine-tune at all?

Fine-tuning adds a training pipeline, a dataset to maintain, an eval suite, a model artefact to version and a serving story. Reach for it when the cheaper options genuinely fall short.

📚
“We'll fine-tune it on our documentation”

This almost never works the way people expect. Fine-tuning shifts the output distribution; it doesn't build a reliable lookup table. The model learns to sound like your docs while still inventing specifics, and updating one fact means retraining. Put the documents in a retrieval index and fine-tune only for the format and behaviour of the answers.

Full fine-tuning

Update every weight, exactly like pretraining but on a small curated dataset with a low learning rate (1e-5 to 5e-5). It gives the most control and costs the most: optimizer state and gradients for every parameter (Chapter 10's memory arithmetic), plus a full model copy per task.

🧠
Catastrophic forgetting

Train hard on 5,000 customer-support conversations and the model gets better at support and measurably worse at everything else — coding, other languages, general reasoning. Mitigations: low learning rate, few epochs, mix in 10–30% general instruction data, use PEFT so most weights stay frozen, and always evaluate on general benchmarks, not just your task.

LoRA: train 0.1% of the weights

The insight: the change a fine-tune makes to a weight matrix is approximately low-rank. So don't learn ΔW (d×d). Learn two thin matrices whose product approximates it:

W' = W + BA        # W frozen (d×d), B is d×r, A is r×d, r ≈ 8–64
                   # trainable params: 2·d·r instead of d²
                   # d=4096, r=16  →  131k instead of 16.8M  (0.8%)

# A is initialised randomly, B to zeros, so the model starts unchanged.
# output scaled by alpha/r, so changing r doesn't change effective strength.

🪡

LoRA

Low-rank deltas on chosen matrices. Adapter is a few MB, training memory is a fraction, and quality is close to full fine-tuning on most tasks.

🗜️

QLoRA

Base weights quantized to 4-bit and frozen; LoRA trained on top in bf16. Fine-tune a 70B model on a single 48 GB GPU — the technique that made fine-tuning accessible.

🎛️

Prefix / prompt tuning

Learn a handful of virtual tokens prepended to the input. Tiny artefacts; generally weaker than LoRA and it eats context length.

🔀
Merge, or keep the adapter separate?

Merge (W ← W + BA) when one task dominates: zero inference overhead, but you now own a full-size model per task. Keep separate when serving many tenants: modern servers hold one base model in memory and apply per-request adapters in the same batch (multi-LoRA), so 100 customers share one GPU. That capability is often the reason to choose LoRA over full fine-tuning in the first place.

The dataset is the whole job

For instruction tuning, quality beats quantity by a wide margin — the LIMA result showed 1,000 excellent examples outperforming 50,000 mediocre ones. Aim for correct, diverse, consistently formatted, and representative of real inputs.

✅ Good dataset

500–5,000 examples · real user phrasings including messy ones · consistent answer format · verified correctness · a held-out set drawn from the same distribution · explicit refusals and edge cases.

❌ Bad dataset

50,000 generated examples nobody read · one phrasing style · answers of wildly varying length and format · no negatives · test examples that leaked from training · all happy-path.

Templates and loss masking

Two mechanical details that quietly decide whether a fine-tune works:

<|system|>You are a support assistant for Acme Cloud. <|user|>My deploy is stuck in "pending". What do I check? <|assistant|>Check three things, in order: 1. Quota — `acme quota show` …

Struck-through tokens are masked out of the loss: you train the model to produce the assistant turn, not to reproduce prompts. And the template must be byte-identical to the one used at inference — a mismatched special token is the most common silent cause of a fine-tune that “did nothing”.

Continued pretraining vs fine-tuning

If your domain's language is unusual — legal filings, clinical notes, chip-design netlists, a proprietary codebase — instruction tuning on a few thousand examples won't fix it. Continued (domain-adaptive) pretraining on billions of in-domain tokens does, and is then followed by instruction tuning. Budget it like a small pretraining run, and mix in general data to limit forgetting.

🧭
The realistic order of attempts

1) Prompt well, with examples. 2) Add retrieval. 3) Build an eval set — before training anything. 4) LoRA fine-tune on a few thousand good examples. 5) Scale the data or move to full fine-tuning if the eval justifies it. 6) Consider continued pretraining only for genuinely unusual domains. Most teams jump to step 4 without step 3 and then cannot tell whether it helped.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Fine-tuning to inject facts. Use retrieval; fine-tune for behaviour.
  • No eval set built before training. Then you cannot tell improvement from regression, and you'll ship on vibes.
  • Training on prompt tokens. The model learns to generate the question as well as the answer, wasting capacity and degrading outputs.
  • A different chat template at inference. Silently destroys most of the gain — check the exact special tokens.
  • Too many epochs. Three epochs on 800 examples will memorise them; eval loss turns up while train loss keeps falling.
  • Only measuring the target task. Check general capability too, or you'll ship a model that forgot how to write code.

Interview drill

Warm-up: Why does LoRA work — what's the assumption?

How a strong answer sounds
That the weight update needed to adapt a pretrained model to a narrow task has low intrinsic rank: the model already has the capabilities, and fine-tuning mostly re-weights and re-routes them rather than learning new structure. So ΔW ≈ BA with r ≪ d captures most of the useful change. Practically it also means the adapter is tiny, B initialised to zero makes training start from the exact base model, and the low-rank form composes — you can swap adapters per request. Where the assumption breaks — genuinely new domains or long continued pretraining — full-rank training does better.

Deeper: Design fine-tuning for 200 enterprise customers who each want a model in their own voice.

How a strong answer sounds
One shared base model plus 200 LoRA adapters of a few megabytes each — never 200 full checkpoints. Serving uses a multi-LoRA-capable engine so requests from different tenants batch together on one GPU with per-request adapter weights, keeping utilization high. Each tenant gets an isolated dataset, its own eval set and a versioned adapter with a rollback path; training is a templated job, so onboarding a customer is data collection rather than engineering. I'd also guard against per-tenant forgetting by mixing in general instruction data, and enforce strict tenancy isolation on both training data and adapter artefacts (Chapters 37, 39). If one customer's volume is huge and latency-critical, merge their adapter into a dedicated copy.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Fine-tune for behaviour and format; use retrieval for facts.
  • LoRA learns a low-rank delta: ~0.1–1% of parameters trained, megabyte-sized adapters, near-full-FT quality.
  • QLoRA quantizes the frozen base so large models fine-tune on a single GPU.
  • A few thousand excellent, consistently templated examples beat tens of thousands of mediocre ones.
  • Always evaluate general capability alongside the target task to catch forgetting.

Check your understanding

Your support bot must answer from a knowledge base that changes weekly. Best approach?

Facts that change belong in an index you can update in seconds, with citations. Fine-tuning would be a weekly retrain that still hallucinates specifics.

With d = 4096 and rank 16, how many parameters does one LoRA-adapted matrix train?

Two matrices of 4096×16 — about 0.8% of the full matrix. That ratio is why adapters are megabytes and training memory collapses.

After fine-tuning on support tickets, your model writes great support replies but its coding ability dropped sharply. What happened?

Training hard on one narrow distribution shifts the model away from everything else. Mix in general data, use fewer epochs and a lower rate, and evaluate general benchmarks before shipping.

Why mask prompt tokens out of the loss during instruction tuning?

Unmasked prompts spend capacity modelling input text, which is not the behaviour you want and measurably weakens the response quality.

You need to serve 50 customer-specific models on limited GPUs. Best design?

Multi-LoRA serving batches requests from different tenants against one shared base, with per-request adapter weights — the standard way to make per-customer models economically viable. Merging them together would just average the behaviours into mush.
