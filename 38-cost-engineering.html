GPU Capacity & Cost Engineering

GPUs are the most expensive infrastructure most companies have ever rented, and the majority of that money buys idle time. Cost engineering for ML is not penny-pinching — it's the difference between a product with viable unit economics and a demo that can't be launched.

  • Build a unit-economics model: cost per token, request and successful outcome
  • Find where utilization is actually lost
  • Choose between on-demand, reserved, spot and owned hardware
  • Plan capacity with headroom, queueing and cold starts in mind
  • Attribute cost per tenant and stop runaway spend

Know your unit

“We spend £180k a month on GPUs” is not a number you can act on. These are:

UnitWhy it's useful
Cost per 1M tokens (in / out separately)Comparable to vendor pricing; shows whether self-hosting is worth it
Cost per requestMaps onto product pricing and per-feature budgets
Cost per active user per monthThe number that decides whether the business model works
Cost per successful outcomeThe honest one — retries, escalations and abandoned sessions are all cost with no value
Cost per experimentMakes research spend visible and comparable to its yield
🎯
Cost per successful outcome changes decisions

A cheap model that answers 70% of tickets correctly may cost more per resolved ticket than an expensive one that answers 92%, once you count escalations to humans. Optimising cost per token while the escalation rate climbs is how teams save £20k of GPU and spend £80k of support salary.

📉
Utilization is a cost metric, not a performance metric

A fleet at 40% utilization is one where 60% of the bill buys nothing. But chasing 95% is also wrong: you need headroom for spikes, failover and deploys. Aim for a target band (say 55–75% at peak) and treat sustained excursions in either direction as an action: scale down, or scale up before the SLO breaks.

🧮
The layered capacity strategy

Reserve your floor (the demand that exists every hour), use on-demand for the daily peak, and put interruptible work — training, evals, batch scoring — on spot so it soaks up whatever is cheap. Most teams get 30–50% off simply by matching the commitment shape to the demand shape, before touching a single model.

Cold start is what makes GPU autoscaling hard

Pulling a container and loading 140 GB of weights takes minutes. Autoscaling that reacts in ten minutes cannot serve a spike that arrives in two. Mitigations: keep a warm pool sized to your typical spike, pre-pull images onto nodes, cache weights on local NVMe, load lazily and stream, and scale on a leading signal (queue depth trend, scheduled traffic patterns) rather than a lagging one.

Attribution and control

🏷️

Tag everything

Team, model, environment, tenant. Untagged spend becomes “platform overhead”, which is where accountability goes to die.

📊

Showback then chargeback

Show teams their cost per model and per feature first. Behaviour usually changes before any money moves.

🚨

Quotas and anomaly alerts

Per-tenant token quotas and spend caps. Alert on a 3× day-over-day jump — a loop, a leaked key or an abusive tenant all look the same on the invoice.

💸
Denial of wallet

An attacker (or a buggy client) who can trigger long generations can multiply your bill without breaking anything. Defences: per-tenant rate and token quotas, a hard max-output-tokens, request timeouts, a spend cap per API key with automatic suspension, and cost anomaly alerts. Treat cost as an availability property — running out of budget takes you down just as effectively as running out of GPUs (Chapter 36).

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Optimising cost per token while the escalation rate rises. Measure cost per successful outcome.
  • Reserving capacity for the peak. Commit to the floor; burst on demand; put interruptible work on spot.
  • Autoscaling on GPU utilization. It saturates instantly and carries no headroom signal — use queue depth.
  • Ignoring development and experiment GPUs. Often the largest single pool of waste, and the least monitored.
  • No per-tenant attribution. You cannot price a product whose cost you cannot attribute.
  • Buying hardware for a workload you haven't measured for six months. A three-year commitment on a three-week trend.

Interview drill

Warm-up: Your GPU bill doubled with flat traffic. Where do you look?

How a strong answer sounds
Something changed in cost per request. I'd check: prompt or context length — a retrieval change that returns more chunks, or a system-prompt edit that broke prefix caching, are the two most common causes and both double input tokens overnight. Then output length, which a prompt or model change can inflate. Then routing: are requests that used to hit the small model now going to the large one, or is a fallback firing constantly? Then infrastructure: replicas scaled up and never scaled down, an idle training cluster, a stuck batch job, or dev instances nobody reaped. Finally abuse: a per-tenant breakdown will show a single account that grew 50×. The fix for the recurrence is per-tenant attribution, a cost-per-request dashboard and an anomaly alert — so the next doubling is a page, not a month-end surprise.

Deeper: Self-host or use a per-token API?

How a strong answer sounds
It's a volume and control question. Below a few hundred million tokens a month, APIs almost always win: no idle capacity, no ops burden, immediate access to newer models. Above that, self-hosting can be several times cheaper per token — if you can keep utilization high, which means steady traffic, good batching and someone to own it. The break-even calculation must include engineering time, spare capacity for failover, and the cost of falling behind on model quality. Non-cost factors often decide it anyway: data residency, latency floors, custom fine-tunes, or a contractual requirement that data never leaves your infrastructure. A common middle path is hosting a small distilled model for the high-volume easy traffic and calling an API for the hard tail — which is the routing argument from Chapter 27 applied to procurement.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Pick a unit — cost per successful outcome is the one that prevents false economies.
  • Most GPU spend buys idle time; utilization is a cost metric with a target band, not a maximum.
  • Match commitment shape to demand shape: reserve the floor, burst on demand, spot the interruptible.
  • Cold starts make GPU autoscaling a leading-indicator problem; keep warm pools.
  • Tag, attribute, quota and alert — cost is an availability property.

Check your understanding

Which single change usually gives the largest cost reduction for an LLM product?

Not invoking the expensive model is worth more than making it cheaper. Quantization and batching then multiply the savings on what remains.

You reserve capacity equal to your peak demand. What's the problem?

Commit to the floor — the demand present every hour — and serve the peak with on-demand capacity, with interruptible work on spot.

Why is GPU utilization a poor autoscaling signal?

Queue depth or queue wait time maps to the latency SLO and gives you warning before users feel anything.

Your cheaper model cut cost per token by 60% but support costs rose. What metric would have caught this?

Escalations, retries and abandoned sessions are cost without value. Only an outcome-denominated metric prices them in.

What is “denial of wallet”?

Nothing breaks, the bill just grows. Per-tenant quotas, output caps, spend limits with automatic suspension and anomaly alerts are the defences.
