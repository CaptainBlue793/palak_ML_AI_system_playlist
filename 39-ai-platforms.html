Multi-Tenant AI Platforms & Gateways

Once a second team wants to call a model, you have a platform whether you planned one or not. The question is whether it's a shared gateway with quotas, routing and attribution — or fifteen teams with their own API keys, their own retry logic and one shared invoice nobody can explain.

  • Design an AI gateway and know what belongs in it
  • Route requests by capability, cost and load, with fallback chains
  • Isolate tenants so one cannot degrade another
  • Offer a self-service path that doesn't become a bottleneck
  • Decide what to build and what to buy

Keep the gateway thin

Every millisecond and every feature you add sits on the critical path of every AI call in the company. Auth, routing, quotas, logging and caching — yes. Business logic, prompt templates, retrieval and agent orchestration — no: those belong to the applications, or they become a shared bottleneck that every team waits on and nobody can change safely.

Fallback chains are a reliability feature

Primary model → secondary model → smaller self-hosted model → cached/templated response → honest error. Each step degrades quality rather than availability, and every fallback must be logged and monitored: a silent fallback that becomes permanent is a quality regression nobody can see. Test the chain deliberately — a fallback path that has never run is a fallback path that doesn't work.

The cache key is a security boundary

A semantic cache that returns tenant A's answer to tenant B is a data breach that looks like a performance optimisation. Include the tenant (and the user's permission set, where retrieval is involved) in every cache key, and write a test that asserts cross-tenant misses. This is a classic, embarrassing and entirely preventable incident.

Self-service: the golden path

1Register the use case: owner, risk tier, data classification, expected volume. Generates the governance record (Chapter 37) rather than a separate document.
2Get credentials scoped to that use case, with default quotas and a cost budget attached.
3Start from a template: a working repo with the SDK, prompt versioning, an eval harness and tracing already wired in.
4Bring an eval set — the platform requires one before production access. This single requirement does more for quality than any review board.
5Ship behind the standard rollout: shadow, canary, guardrails, automatic rollback (Chapter 33) — provided by the platform, not reimplemented per team.
6Get a dashboard for free: latency, cost, quality samples, fallback rate, quota headroom, per tenant.
🛤️
Make the safe path the easy path

Platform teams fail when they act as gatekeepers — a review queue that teams route around. They succeed when the paved road is genuinely faster than doing it yourself: a template that works in ten minutes, observability you'd otherwise have to build, and quotas that protect you from your own bugs. Governance that arrives as a side effect of the golden path is the only kind that scales.

Build or buy

Buy / adopt

Gateways, tracing, prompt management, eval tooling and vector databases are commodity now. Buying gets you a working platform this quarter — and the integration work is still real, so budget for it.

Build

Build what encodes your policy: tenancy model, routing rules, quota semantics, governance records, and the glue to your identity and billing systems. These are the parts no vendor can know.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • A thick gateway. Prompts and business logic inside it make every team wait on the platform team.
  • No tenant in the cache key. A cross-tenant leak presented as a latency improvement.
  • Shared pool with no quotas. One team's batch job takes the interactive SLO down at 2pm.
  • Untested fallback chains. Discovered to be broken during the outage they exist for.
  • Platform as gatekeeper. Teams route around it, and you lose the visibility that was the point.
  • No cost attribution from day one. Retrofitting tags across 14 services is far harder than starting with them.

Interview drill

Warm-up: Why put an AI gateway in front of a vendor API rather than letting each service call it directly?

How a strong answer sounds
Because the cross-cutting concerns have to live somewhere: authentication and tenancy, per-team quotas and spend caps, caching, consistent logging and redaction for audit, safety filtering, and cost attribution. It also buys portability — switching or adding a provider becomes a config change rather than a change in every service — and it gives you one place to implement fallbacks when a provider degrades. The discipline is keeping it thin: it should be latency-cheap and free of business logic, or it becomes the bottleneck it was meant to remove.

Deeper: Design multi-tenancy for an AI platform serving 200 enterprise customers.

How a strong answer sounds
Isolation on six axes. Data: per-tenant retrieval indexes or strict ACL filters applied during search, with an automated cross-tenant test. Models: one shared base with per-tenant LoRA adapters served in the same batch, adapter id bound to the tenant and asserted at load (Chapter 17). Capacity: priority classes with per-tenant token quotas and admission control so no tenant can starve another, plus dedicated capacity for the few that pay for it. Cache: tenant and permission set in every key. Logs: scoped access, redaction, per-tenant retention honouring their contract. Cost: every request and GPU-second tagged for attribution and billing. On top of that, per-tenant dashboards and quota headroom alerts, because the support question “why is it slow for us?” must be answerable in one click. And the isolation properties get automated tests, because they are security properties, not performance ones.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A thin gateway centralises auth, quotas, routing, caching, logging and cost attribution.
  • Routing by capability and difficulty is the biggest cost lever; fallback chains are a reliability feature.
  • Isolate data, adapters, capacity, cache, logs and cost — and test the isolation.
  • A golden path with a mandatory eval set beats a review board.
  • Buy commodity tooling; build the parts that encode your tenancy, policy and billing.

Check your understanding

Which belongs in the AI gateway?

Cross-cutting infrastructure concerns belong in the gateway; application logic belongs in applications, or the platform becomes everyone's bottleneck.

Your semantic cache returns a stored answer to a different customer. What went wrong?

A cross-tenant cache hit is a data breach. Tenant identity belongs in every key, with an automated test asserting cross-tenant misses.

One team's nightly batch job pushes interactive p95 latency over the SLO. What's the fix?

Interactive and batch traffic should never compete on equal terms. Separate queues with weights, or separate pools, make it structural rather than social.

Why require an eval set before granting production access?

Teams without evals ship on anecdote and cannot safely change anything afterwards. Requiring one at the door does more than any review meeting.

What is the risk of an untested fallback chain?

Exercise fallbacks deliberately (game days, forced failures) and alert on fallback rate — a silent permanent fallback is a quality regression with no error attached.
