The Math You Actually Need

You do not need a maths degree to build ML systems. You need four ideas — vectors, matrices, derivatives and probability — held as pictures rather than symbols. This chapter builds those pictures, and you can drag most of them.

  • Read a dot product as “how much do these two things agree?”
  • See a matrix as a machine that transforms vectors, and get its shapes right
  • Understand a derivative as a slope and a gradient as a direction downhill
  • Interpret probabilities, likelihood and cross-entropy loss
  • Debug the error you will hit most: a shape mismatch

Everything is a vector

A vector is just an ordered list of numbers. A user is [age, days_since_signup, orders, avg_basket]. A word is a list of 1,536 numbers produced by an embedding model. A 224×224 colour image is 150,528 numbers. Once something is a vector, geometry applies to it — and geometry is where the intuition lives.

🧭
Analogy: directions on a map

Two vectors pointing the same way are “about the same thing”, even if one is much longer. That is the whole idea behind embedding similarity: direction carries meaning, length carries intensity. “king” and “queen” point in similar directions; “king” and “photosynthesis” do not.

The dot product: agreement in one number

The dot product multiplies matching components and adds them up: a·b = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + …. Big positive means “these agree”, zero means “unrelated”, negative means “they oppose”. Divide by both lengths and you get cosine similarity, which ignores magnitude and keeps only direction — the score behind every vector database in Chapter 20.

💡
Where you'll meet this

A single neuron computes dot(weights, inputs) + bias. Attention scores a query against every key with a dot product. A vector index ranks documents by cosine similarity. Learn to read a·b as “how strongly does this pattern fire on this input?” and half of deep learning stops looking like symbols.

Matrices: many dot products at once

A matrix is a stack of vectors, and multiplying by one is doing a whole batch of dot products in a single operation. That's it. A linear layer with 512 inputs and 256 outputs is a 512×256 matrix: each of the 256 output numbers is one dot product against the input.

📐
The rule that causes 80% of your errors

For A·B, the inner dimensions must match: (m×k)·(k×n) → (m×n). When a stack trace says mat1 and mat2 shapes cannot be multiplied (32x512 and 256x512), you transposed something or your batch dimension is in the wrong place. Read shapes left to right and say them out loud.

Derivatives: which way is downhill?

Training is a search for parameters that make the loss small. The derivative tells you the slope of the loss at your current point: if it is positive, moving right increases loss, so move left. A gradient is just that slope computed for millions of parameters at once — a vector pointing in the direction of steepest increase, which you then step against.

The update is w ← w − η·dL/dw. Here η = 0.15. Notice the steps get smaller near the bottom automatically — because the gradient itself shrinks.

⛰️
The fog analogy

You're on a hillside in thick fog and want the valley. You can't see the landscape, but you can feel the slope under your feet. So you step downhill, re-feel, step again. That's gradient descent. The learning rate is your stride: too small and you're there all week, too large and you stride straight over the valley onto the opposite slope.

The chain rule, in one line

Networks are functions of functions: loss(head(block(embed(x)))). The chain rule says the derivative of a composition is the product of the derivatives — so you can compute the gradient for a 100-layer network by multiplying local slopes backwards from the loss. That is backpropagation (Chapter 8), and it is why deep learning is computationally feasible at all.

# forward
z = w * x        # local: dz/dw = x
a = relu(z)      # local: da/dz = 1 if z > 0 else 0
L = (a - y)**2   # local: dL/da = 2(a - y)

# backward — multiply the local slopes
dL/dw = 2(a - y) * (1 if z > 0 else 0) * x

Probability: models output beliefs, not answers

A classifier does not say “spam”. It says P(spam | this email) = 0.93. Everything downstream — thresholds, calibration, expected cost — is probability. Four ideas cover nearly all of it.

🎲

Distribution

click to flip
How probability is spread over outcomes. A model's output layer is a distribution: softmax over 50,000 tokens, or one number for a yes/no.
📊

Expectation

click to flip
The average outcome, weighted by probability. Expected cost = P(fraud)×loss − (1−P)×friction. This is how a probability becomes a decision.
🔁

Conditional / Bayes

click to flip
P(A|B) — belief after seeing evidence. Bayes: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. Rare things stay rare: a 99%-accurate test for a 1-in-10,000 disease is usually wrong when positive.
🌀

Entropy

click to flip
How uncertain a distribution is. Low entropy = confident. Cross-entropy measures how far your predicted distribution is from the true one — the loss for nearly every classifier and every LLM.

Cross-entropy: the loss you'll use most

For classification, the loss for one example is −log(p), where p is the probability your model assigned to the correct answer. Confidently right costs nothing; confidently wrong costs enormously. Drag the slider and watch the asymmetry.

A language model's perplexity is e^(mean cross-entropy) — roughly “how many equally-likely options is the model effectively choosing between?”. Perplexity 1 means certainty; perplexity 50,000 means it has learned nothing.

🧮
Why logs are everywhere

Probabilities multiply and underflow fast: 1,000 tokens at p=0.5 each is 10⁻³⁰¹. Logs turn multiplication into addition, keep numbers in a sane range, and make gradients well-behaved. Whenever you see logits, log_softmax or logsumexp, that's the reason.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Comparing raw dot products across vectors of different lengths. A long, mediocre vector can beat a short, perfect one. Normalize, or use cosine, when you mean “similar”.
  • Feeding probabilities where logits are expected. CrossEntropyLoss in most frameworks applies its own softmax; passing softmaxed values applies it twice and silently kills training.
  • Averaging probabilities in log space or vice versa. The mean of logs is a geometric mean; mixing the two quietly changes what you are measuring.
  • Assuming a high-accuracy test means a high-probability positive. Base rates dominate: with a 1-in-10,000 event and a 1% false-positive rate, most positives are false. Say it in expected counts, not percentages.
🎤
Interview drill

Warm-up: Explain cosine similarity to a product manager in two sentences, and say when it is the wrong choice.

How a strong answer sounds
It measures whether two items point the same way in feature space, ignoring how big they are — so a short document and a long one about the same topic score as similar. It's the wrong choice when magnitude carries meaning (counts, spend, intensity) or when embeddings are already normalized and the extra division just costs compute — in which case a plain dot product is identical and faster.

Numeracy: A fraud model outputs P(fraud)=0.02 for a £500 transaction. Blocking a legitimate customer costs you about £8 of goodwill; letting fraud through costs the full £500. Block or allow?

How a strong answer sounds
Expected cost of allowing = 0.02 × 500 = £10. Expected cost of blocking = 0.98 × 8 ≈ £7.84. Block — but only just, so the decision is sensitive to calibration: if the model's 2% is really 1.5%, allowing wins. This is exactly why calibration (Chapter 7) matters more than raw AUC when a probability feeds a monetary decision, and why thresholds get tuned per amount band rather than set globally.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A dot product measures agreement; cosine similarity measures agreement ignoring magnitude.
  • A matrix multiply is a batch of dot products — get the inner dimensions to match and most bugs vanish.
  • A gradient points uphill; training steps against it, with the learning rate as stride length.
  • Cross-entropy −log(p) punishes confident mistakes brutally and is the default classification loss.
  • Model outputs are probabilities; turning them into decisions requires expected cost, not just a threshold.

Check your understanding

Two embedding vectors have a dot product of 0. What does that tell you?

Zero dot product means a 90° angle: the vectors share no component. Opposite directions give a large negative dot product.

You multiply a batch X of shape (64, 300) by weights W. What shape must W have to produce (64, 128)?

Inner dimensions must match: (64×300)·(300×128) → (64×128). The batch dimension passes through untouched.

The gradient of the loss with respect to a weight is +4.0 and the learning rate is 0.1. What is the update?

w ← w − η·g = w − 0.1×4.0. A positive gradient means increasing the weight increases loss, so you step the other way.

Your classifier assigns probability 0.01 to the correct class. Roughly what is the cross-entropy loss for that example?

−ln(0.01) ≈ 4.6. Confidently wrong is enormously expensive — which is why a few mislabelled examples can dominate a training batch.

A disease affects 1 in 10,000 people. A test has a 1% false-positive rate and catches every true case. You test positive. Roughly what is the chance you have it?

Per million people: 100 true cases, ~10,000 false positives. So ≈100/10,100 ≈ 1%. Base rates dominate — the same arithmetic explains why a “99% accurate” fraud or moderation model can still drown reviewers in false alarms.
