Case Study: Self-Driving Perception

Turn eight cameras, a lidar and five radars into a coherent picture of the world, forty times a second, on a computer bolted into a car, with a latency budget measured in tens of milliseconds and a failure mode that is not a bad recommendation. Everything you have learned about iterating quickly meets a domain where being wrong occasionally is not an acceptable operating point.

  • Design a multi-sensor, multi-task perception stack under a hard real-time budget
  • Build the data engine that mines the long tail from a fleet
  • Reason about edge compute, determinism and redundancy
  • Construct a safety argument that survives statistical scrutiny
  • Understand why the last 1% of scenarios takes 99% of the effort

🎯
Metrics

Safety-critical: missed detections of vulnerable road users per million miles, sliced by lighting, weather and occlusion; false-brake events per thousand miles. Perception quality: detection and tracking accuracy by range band and object class, velocity error, prediction displacement error at 3 s. System: end-to-end latency at p99.9 (not p95 — the tail is the whole point), frame drop rate, thermal throttling incidents. Programme: miles per critical intervention, and the rate at which newly discovered failure modes are closed.

⚖️
Why every metric in this chapter is a tail metric

A recommender that is 95% accurate is excellent. A perception stack that misses 5% of pedestrians is a lethal product. The distributions you care about are entirely in the tail: the p99.9 of latency, the rarest 0.1% of scenes, the one lighting condition your training set under-represents. This inverts the usual engineering economics — average-case improvements are nearly worthless, and almost all the effort goes into finding and fixing cases that occur once every few hundred thousand miles.

2 · The perception stack

One frame, sensors to plannerStep-through
  1. Sensors run at different rates and none of them are truly simultaneous. Hardware-triggered capture with a shared clock gives every measurement a trustworthy timestamp. At 30 m/s, a 10 ms timing error is a 30 cm position error — enough to change whether a pedestrian is in your lane.
  2. Extrinsics drift with temperature, potholes and a service technician leaning on a mirror. Online calibration monitoring runs continuously, because a slowly rotating camera degrades fusion quality in a way that looks exactly like a model regression and is not.
  3. Each camera runs a shared-weight backbone producing features rather than boxes. Extracting features first and fusing later is what makes multi-camera reasoning possible: an object crossing between two cameras must not become two objects.
  4. Project features into a shared top-down representation using the calibrated geometry, and fuse camera, lidar and radar there. This is the architectural heart of a modern stack: one consistent space where all sensors and all tasks agree, rather than per-camera detections stitched together afterwards with heuristics.
  5. Detection, lane geometry, drivable space, traffic controls, occupancy — all from the same features. Necessary for the compute budget, and useful for accuracy through shared representation. The cost is coupling: a change that helps one head can regress another, so every head is a release gate.
  6. Per-frame detections become persistent objects with velocity and identity, then the stack predicts several seconds of plausible futures with probabilities. A cyclist's intent matters far more to the planner than a tight bounding box, and tracking is where flicker — an object detected, lost, redetected — turns into phantom braking.
  7. The planner needs calibrated confidence and explicit occlusion reasoning: "there may be something behind that van" is an output, not a gap. A perception stack that reports only what it saw forces the planner to assume the world is fully observed, which it never is.
  8. The vehicle generates terabytes an hour and can upload a tiny fraction. On-board triggers select the interesting seconds — disagreement between sensors, low-confidence detections, driver interventions, near-misses, novel scenes — and those clips are the fuel for everything in the next section.

Auto-labelling: the offline model is allowed to cheat

The on-vehicle model must run causally in 45 ms. The labelling model faces neither constraint: it can see the future of the clip, run a hundred times slower, use a far larger network, and fuse lidar returns the production stack may not have. Running that model over a clip produces labels better than a human can draw — especially for velocity, occluded extents and distant objects — and humans then audit and correct a sample instead of drawing every box.

This is knowledge distillation with a temporal advantage (Chapter 27), and it is the single biggest lever on labelling cost in the industry. The trap is a feedback loop: if auto-labels come from a teacher trained on the same data, you reinforce shared blind spots. Guard it with a human-audited holdout that never enters the auto-label loop.

4 · Deep dive: the on-vehicle budget

StageBudgetConstraint
Capture and ISP~8 msFixed by hardware; sets the floor on everything downstream
Backbones (8 cameras)~18 msThe dominant cost; int8 quantized, fused kernels, no dynamic shapes
BEV fusion + heads~12 msShared trunk keeps this affordable
Tracking + prediction~7 msMostly CPU; must be deterministic for replay
Planner + control~25 msOutside this chapter, but it shares the 100 ms sense-to-act budget
Thermal headroom~15%A stack that only fits in winter is a stack that does not fit

Three constraints have no analogue in cloud serving. Determinism: the same inputs must produce the same outputs, or a replay cannot reproduce an incident and you cannot prove a fix. No batching across requests: there is one vehicle, so latency cannot be traded for throughput. Degradation, not failure: if a camera fails, the stack must keep operating with reduced capability and tell the planner the field of view has shrunk.

5 · Deep dive: the safety argument

The statisticsSerious injury occurs roughly once per tens of millions of miles of human driving. Demonstrating a superior rate by direct observation alone requires billions of miles — decades of fleet time. No programme can validate by road miles alone, so the argument has to be built from several weaker pieces.
SimulationMillions of scenario variations per release, with the perturbations that matter: the pedestrian one step later, the van parked half a metre further out. The known weakness is that simulation mostly tests the situations you thought to write down.
ResimulationReplay real logged clips against the new stack and diff the outputs. Catches regressions on the exact scenes that previously caused trouble, and only works if the stack is deterministic.
Shadow modeRun the new stack on fleet vehicles without control authority and compare its decisions with the human's. Enormous statistical power with zero exposure — the closest thing to free evidence in this domain.
Operational design domainState exactly where the system is claimed to work: which roads, speeds, weather, lighting. Most of the safety case is a bounded claim plus reliable detection of leaving those bounds.
Regression disciplineEvery discovered failure becomes a permanent test case. The programme's real velocity metric is how fast newly found failure modes get closed, not how good the headline benchmark looks (Chapter 32).

🎤
Interview drill

Follow-up: “Your detection benchmark improved 3% but the fleet sees more phantom braking. How is that possible?”

How a strong answer sounds
Because per-frame detection accuracy is not what causes phantom braking — temporal instability is. A model can detect an object slightly better on average while flickering more: detected, lost for two frames, redetected as a new track. Downstream, that reads as an object appearing suddenly at close range, and the planner brakes. The benchmark has no notion of identity persistence, so this is invisible to it. I would look at track-level metrics — identity switches, track fragmentation, velocity stability — and at the distribution rather than the mean, because the aggregate gain probably comes from easy mid-range objects while the regression is at the edges of the field of view or on partially occluded ones. The fix path is resimulation against the clips that produced the braking events, then adding temporal-stability terms to both the training objective and the release gates. The general lesson: the metric that moves the product is rarely the metric the model was trained on, and in this domain the gap is dangerous rather than merely annoying.

Follow-up: “How would you prove the new release is safer than the old one?”

How a strong answer sounds
Not by road miles — the events are too rare, and demonstrating a meaningful difference by direct observation would take billions of miles. I would build a layered argument. Resimulation across the full regression library, so I can show that every previously known failure is still fixed and diff every behavioural change on real logged scenes. Simulation across millions of perturbed scenarios, with coverage reported by scenario family so the gaps are explicit rather than implied. Shadow mode on the fleet: run the new stack without control authority and compare its decisions against the old stack and the human driver, which gives real statistical power at zero exposure. Then component-level gates per object class, range band and weather condition, because an aggregate improvement can hide a regression on pedestrians at night. And finally a staged rollout with a clearly stated operational design domain and a defined trigger for pulling it back. No single one of those proves safety; together they form an argument that a regulator, and an engineer, can actually interrogate.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Fuse sensor features in one shared bird's-eye space rather than stitching per-camera detections together afterwards.
  • The data engine — triggers, mining, auto-labelling, targeted collection — is the real product; the model architecture is comparatively commoditised.
  • An offline auto-labelling model may use the future and unlimited compute, which makes labels better and cheaper than human ones.
  • On-vehicle serving adds determinism, thermal limits and graceful degradation to the usual latency constraints.
  • Safety cannot be validated by road miles; it is argued from resimulation, simulation, shadow mode and a bounded operational domain.

Check your understanding

Why fuse sensors in a shared bird's-eye-view space rather than merging per-camera detections?

Late fusion of per-camera boxes needs heuristics to reconcile disagreements, and those heuristics are where the long-tail failures live.

Why can an offline auto-labelling model produce better labels than the on-vehicle model?

The production model must be causal and finish in 45 ms. The labeller has neither constraint — which is why its velocity and occlusion labels beat what a human would draw.

Why is determinism a hard requirement on the vehicle?

Resimulation is the backbone of the safety argument, and it only works if identical inputs give identical outputs.

Why can safety not be validated by driving enough miles?

Hence the layered argument: resimulation, simulation, shadow mode, component gates and a bounded operational design domain.

Detection accuracy improved but phantom braking increased. Most likely cause?

Per-frame metrics are blind to identity persistence. Track fragmentation and identity switches are what the planner actually reacts to.
