Case Study: E-commerce Search

Design search for a marketplace with 200 million listings and 40 million searches a day. Unlike a feed, the user has told you exactly what they want — and unlike web search, the “right” answer depends on price, stock, delivery, seller quality and margin as much as on relevance.

  • Build a query-understanding pipeline that handles typos, intent and attributes
  • Combine lexical, vector and structured retrieval with hard filters
  • Train a learning-to-rank model on biased click data
  • Balance relevance against business objectives without corrupting either
  • Handle zero-result queries, stock churn and the long tail

🎯
Metrics

Primary (online): revenue per search session, with conversion rate as a secondary. Offline: NDCG@10 against human-judged relevance for head queries, plus click-model-based NDCG for the tail. Guardrails: zero-result rate, p95 latency, share of impressions to sponsored listings, new-seller exposure, and returns rate (a proxy for “the customer bought the wrong thing”).
Baseline: BM25 on title and attributes, sorted by a static popularity prior. Surprisingly hard to beat on head queries; easy to beat on the tail.

2 · Query understanding

NormaliseLowercase, strip punctuation, expand units (13in → 13 inch), handle plurals. Cheap and high-yield.
Spell correctLearned from query logs: the corrections users make themselves are the best training data available (ipone → iphone). Never “correct” a real brand into a common word.
Segment & tagNamed-entity tagging over the query: red nike running shoes size 9 → colour=red, brand=nike, category=running-shoes, size=9. This turns free text into structured filters.
Intent classifyNavigational (a specific product), broad (“gifts for dad”), or attribute-driven. Intent changes the whole ranking strategy — a navigational query wants one exact result, not diversity.
ExpandSynonyms from co-click data (trainers ≈ sneakers), and controlled query relaxation when recall is low. Expansion is where precision goes to die, so gate it on retrieved-result count.
RewriteFor the tail, an embedding of the whole query carries meaning that token matching loses — which is exactly where hybrid retrieval earns its place.
🔤
Tagging the query is worth more than a better ranker

Once size 9 is a structured filter rather than two tokens, you stop returning size-11 shoes that happen to mention “9” in the description. Attribute extraction converts fuzzy text matching into exact constraints, and on marketplaces it typically moves conversion more than any ranking model change — because the failures it removes are the ones that make users leave.

Fuse with reciprocal rank fusion (Chapter 20), then apply hard business filters: in stock, ships to this country, not blocked, seller active. Hard filters run inside retrieval, never afterwards — post-filtering a selective constraint leaves you with three results from the wrong neighbourhood.

4 · Learning to rank

A gradient-boosted ranker (LambdaMART-style) over ~200 features remains extremely competitive here, with a neural ranker as the challenger for the tail where text understanding matters most.

Query–item features

BM25 scores per field, embedding similarity, attribute match count, exact-title match, category agreement.

Item features

Historical CTR and conversion (position-debiased), price versus category median, seller rating, delivery speed, return rate, stock depth, recency.

User & context features

Price sensitivity, brand affinity, device, locale, session history, time of day.

🎣
Your labels are clicks, and clicks are biased

Position bias dominates (Chapter 30), and there's a second trap specific to commerce: the historical CTR feature. An item shown often gets clicks, so its CTR rises, so it ranks higher. Untreated, the ranker learns to reproduce yesterday's ranking. Controls: position-debias the labels with propensities estimated from randomised swaps; compute CTR features out-of-time and shrink them toward the category prior for low-volume items; and use purchases, not clicks, as the primary label wherever volume allows — they're rarer but far less gameable.

6 · Deep dive: the tail and zero results

70% of queries are seen once a week or less

Head queries have abundant click data and are largely solved. The tail has none — no CTR features, no co-click synonyms — so it depends entirely on text and attribute understanding. That's where embeddings, query tagging and relaxation earn their keep, and where offline NDCG on head queries will tell you nothing.

Zero results are a product failure

Track the zero-result rate as a first-class metric. Recovery ladder: drop the least important attribute filter, relax to the parent category, fall back to the vector leg alone, then show “similar items” with an honest explanation. Never show an empty page — and log every case, because the recovery path is also your best source of missing-synonym data.

📦
Selling an out-of-stock item is worse than not ranking it

Stock changes far faster than any reindex cadence, so stock must be a runtime lookup, not a static index field. The common pattern: retrieve generously, then apply availability at ranking time using a fast key-value read, and treat a stale “in stock” as a bug with a defined SLA. A ranking system that is 2% more relevant and sells unavailable items loses.

🎤
Interview drill

Follow-up: “Conversion improved but returns went up 15%.” What happened?

How a strong answer sounds
The ranker learned to surface items people buy, not items that satisfy them — typically by favouring cheap items, misleading titles or wrong-variant matches (size, colour, compatibility). Clicks and purchases are both short-horizon signals; returns are the delayed truth. I'd add returns and post-purchase signals into the training objective as a negative head, check whether attribute matching is failing for variant-heavy categories, slice returns by query class and seller, and add returns rate as a launch guardrail. The general lesson: when a metric improves and a slower one degrades, you've usually optimised a proxy past the point where it tracks the outcome.

Follow-up: “Why not rank everything by conversion probability?”

How a strong answer sounds
Because conversion probability is estimated from data the current ranker generated, so it encodes position bias and popularity feedback — high-exposure items look convertible because they were shown. It also ignores the long tail, where there's no data at all, and it optimises a single session rather than the relationship: cheap, high-conversion items can raise returns and erode trust. A practical ranker predicts several outcomes — click, purchase, return, satisfaction — combines them with weights that are a product decision, and is trained on debiased labels with guardrails on the things conversion doesn't see.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Query understanding — tagging attributes into hard filters — often beats ranking improvements.
  • Hybrid lexical + vector retrieval with structured filters applied inside the search.
  • Debias click labels and shrink CTR features, or the ranker relearns yesterday's ranking.
  • Business objectives belong in an explicit weighted blend with guardrails, not hidden in the label.
  • Stock and price are runtime lookups; zero-result rate and tail slices are first-class metrics.

Check your understanding

Why apply “in stock” as a runtime lookup rather than an index field?

Freshness requirements differ per field. Fast-changing attributes belong in a low-latency store read at ranking time.

Your ranker uses historical CTR as a feature. What's the danger?

A self-reinforcing loop. Debias by position, compute out-of-time, and shrink low-volume items toward the category prior.

70% of queries are long tail with no click history. Which investment helps them most?

Behavioural features don't exist for the tail. Understanding the query text and matching on semantics and attributes is all you have.

Offline NDCG improved 4% but the A/B test showed no revenue change. Most likely explanation?

Human-judged sets skew to head queries, and relevance gains on results users were already going to buy don't move revenue. Slice by query class before believing an aggregate offline gain.

What's the right response to a zero-result query?

Zero results are a lost session and a free signal about missing synonyms or coverage. The recovery ladder salvages the visit and the log improves tomorrow's retrieval.
