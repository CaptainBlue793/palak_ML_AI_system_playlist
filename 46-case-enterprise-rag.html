Case Study: Enterprise RAG Assistant

Ten million documents across SharePoint, Confluence, Jira, Slack, Drive and a ticket system. Sixty thousand employees, each of whom may see a different subset of them. Answers must cite their sources, must never surface a document the asker is not allowed to read, and must be right often enough that people stop searching by hand.

  • Design the connector, parsing and incremental indexing pipeline
  • Enforce per-user permissions inside retrieval, not after it
  • Keep a 10M-document index fresh without rebuilding it
  • Ground answers in citations and abstain when evidence is thin
  • Evaluate a system whose ground truth lives in people's heads

In scope: connectors, indexing, permission-aware retrieval, grounding, evaluation, cost. Out of scope: training a base model (Chapter 52), the chat serving fleet itself (Chapter 45), records-management policy.

🎯
Metrics

Primary: task deflection — questions answered without opening a ticket or interrupting a colleague, measured by a follow-up prompt and by ticket volume in the covered categories. Quality: answer correctness and citation faithfulness on a graded set, retrieval recall@20 on annotated queries, abstention rate. Guardrails: zero permission leaks (a hard gate, not a metric to trade), p95 latency, index staleness, cost per query.

🚫
The one requirement that is not a trade-off

Every other number here is negotiable under pressure: latency, freshness, cost, even accuracy. Permission correctness is not. A single retrieved snippet from an unreleased earnings memo, a performance review or a document under legal hold is an incident that ends the project — and unlike a wrong answer, users cannot detect it, so it can run for months undiscovered. Design the retrieval path so that a leak requires a bug in one place, rather than requiring every one of five layers to work.

2 · Ingestion and indexing

From a SharePoint edit to a retrievable chunkStep-through
  1. Each system gets a first-class connector using its own change API — SharePoint webhooks, Confluence events, Drive push notifications — with a slow reconciliation poll behind it to catch what webhooks drop. A generic crawler would re-read 10M documents to find the 40,000 that changed today.
  2. Every event lands on a durable queue keyed by document id, so bursts — a bulk migration, a permission sweep — are absorbed rather than dropped. Deduplicate by id and version: a document saved eight times in a minute should be indexed once.
  3. The unglamorous 60% of the work: PDFs with tables, scans needing OCR, 400-slide decks, spreadsheets, wiki macros. Chunk on structure — headings and sections — not on a fixed character count, and carry the document title plus the heading path into every chunk's text so a chunk is interpretable alone.
  4. A document that fails to parse is invisible in retrieval and indistinguishable from one that does not exist. It goes to a dead-letter queue with a dashboard and an owner — “we have no answer” is far more often a parse failure than a retrieval failure.
  5. Hash each chunk's text: an edit to page 3 of a 90-page document changes two chunks, not ninety. Embedding is batched on GPU, and the model version is stamped on every vector so a migration can proceed chunk by chunk (Chapter 20).
  6. ACLs change far more often than content, and a re-share must take effect in minutes without re-embedding anything. Permissions get their own feed into their own store, joined at query time — see the next section.
  7. Upsert by chunk id into a vector index and a lexical index that share document metadata: id, source, URL, title, timestamps, ACL tokens, sensitivity label. Both are needed — dense retrieval handles paraphrase, BM25 handles the error code, the project codename and the person's surname (Chapter 21).

🧾
ACL tokens: the shape that actually scales

Store on each chunk a small set of opaque strings — grp:eng-platform, grp:all-staff, usr:1042 — that grant read access, plus a deny set for the rare explicit exclusion. At query time, resolve the asker's group memberships (cached, a few milliseconds) into the same token vocabulary and push allow ∩ user ≠ ∅ AND deny ∩ user = ∅ into the index as a pre-filter. The index applies it during traversal, so the top 20 it returns are already 20 readable chunks.

Two details make or break it. Group expansion must be transitive — nested groups are where leaks hide. And the mirror must be recent: revocation lag is the window in which a former team member still gets answers, so drive it from the change feed and alert on mirror lag, not only on indexing lag.

The asymmetry is the design driver: permission changes outnumber content changes roughly four to one and must land eight times faster, which is exactly why ACLs are not baked into the embedded chunk.

🕰️
Stale beats wrong, and conflicting versions beat both

The commonest complaint in enterprise RAG is not hallucination — it is confidently citing the 2019 policy when the 2024 one exists, because both are in the corpus and the old one happens to be better written. Fixes, in order of effect: index last_modified and boost recency for policy-shaped sources; detect near-duplicate chunks across versions and keep the newest; propagate source deprecation, so an archived space drops out of retrieval rather than merely ranking lower; and surface dates in the citation so the reader can see what they are being told. Retrieval-quality work that ignores document versioning plateaus early.

5 · Grounding, citations and abstention

RetrieveHybrid search with the ACL pre-filter gives ~60 candidates; a cross-encoder reranker cuts them to the top 8. The reranker is where most of the quality comes from, and the second-biggest latency line (Chapter 21).
AssembleChunks are fenced as untrusted data carrying source id, title, URL and date — never concatenated into the instruction text. A document in the corpus is a prompt-injection vector, and some of yours were written by vendors (Chapter 36).
GenerateAnswer with an inline citation per claim, drawn only from the provided chunks. Structured output makes citations machine-checkable rather than decorative.
VerifyA cheap entailment check: does each cited chunk actually support the sentence attached to it? Unsupported sentences are dropped, or the answer is downgraded to “here is what I found”.
AbstainIf reranker scores are all low, say so and show the closest documents. A confident wrong answer about a security policy costs more than an admission of ignorance — abstention rate is a metric to tune, not to minimise.

🎤
Interview drill

Follow-up: “A user says the assistant can't find a document they can clearly see in SharePoint.” Walk me through it.

How a strong answer sounds
I would bisect the pipeline with the document id. Is it in the index at all? If not, did the change feed ever see it — connector health, webhook delivery, reconciliation lag — or did it fail parsing and land in the DLQ? That covers most reports, and it is an ingestion bug, not a retrieval one. If it is indexed, does the query retrieve it for a user with full access? If it does for them and not for the reporter, it is the permission path: ACL tokens on the chunk versus the user's resolved groups, usually a nested group that is not expanded transitively, or a stale mirror. If it retrieves for nobody, it is genuine retrieval quality — a lexical match the dense retriever misses, a chunk that lost its heading context, or a reranker burying it. I would want a per-document debug view showing exactly these stages, because this question gets asked weekly and should not need an engineer.

Follow-up: “Why not just build one index per user?”

How a strong answer sounds
It is the most obviously correct design and it does not survive the numbers. Sixty thousand users against a corpus where most documents are readable by thousands of people means duplicating storage and index-build cost tens of times over, and it turns every permission change into a per-user reindex — at 150k permission changes a day that becomes the dominant workload. It also destroys memory locality: one shared graph in RAM becomes sixty thousand small ones. I would use it only for genuinely isolated enclaves — legal hold, a regulated subsidiary — where isolation is the requirement and the corpus is small. For everyone else, ACL tokens filtered inside the index give the same correctness at a fraction of the cost, provided group expansion is transitive and mirror lag is monitored.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Permissions belong inside the index traversal — post-filtering silently destroys recall and leaves enforcement in application code.
  • ACLs change far more often than content, so mirror them separately and never re-embed to apply them.
  • Parsing and connector coverage, not model quality, decide how much of the corpus the assistant can answer from at all.
  • Document versioning matters as much as retrieval: the classic enterprise failure is a confident citation of a superseded policy.
  • Split every evaluation into retrieval and generation, and treat zero permission leaks as a gate rather than a metric.

Check your understanding

Why is filtering search results by permission after retrieval a poor design?

If a user can read 2% of the corpus, an unfiltered top-20 yields roughly zero readable results. You must over-fetch enormously or, better, push the constraint into the index traversal.

Why are ACLs kept out of the embedded chunk and mirrored separately?

150k permission changes a day against 40k content edits, with a tighter lag target. Coupling them would make every re-share an embedding job.

Users report the assistant “doesn't know about” a whole class of internal documents. Most likely cause?

Coverage gaps look exactly like quality gaps from the outside. A document that failed to parse is indistinguishable from one that does not exist — which is why the DLQ needs a dashboard and an owner.

The assistant confidently cites a 2019 policy that was superseded in 2024. What fixes this most directly?

Both documents are genuinely in the corpus and both are relevant to the query. This is a corpus-curation problem wearing a retrieval costume.

Why must retrieved documents be fenced as untrusted data?

Any wiki page, ticket comment or attached PDF is user-supplied content. Treat it as data, and never let it authorise a tool call or override policy.
