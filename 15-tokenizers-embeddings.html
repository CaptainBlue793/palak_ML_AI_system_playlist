Tokenizers & Embeddings

Before a model sees text it sees integers, and before it reasons it looks those integers up in a table. Tokenization decides your costs, your multilingual fairness and several famous model failures. Embeddings turn the result into geometry — the substrate of every retrieval system you'll build.

  • Explain byte-pair encoding and why subwords beat words and characters
  • Predict tokenizer pathologies: numbers, code, non-English text
  • Read embedding geometry and compute similarity correctly
  • Describe how embedding models are trained with contrastive objectives
  • Budget the storage and re-embedding cost of a vector corpus

Why tokens exist

You need a fixed vocabulary of symbols. Two obvious options both fail:

Whole words

Vocabulary is unbounded (names, typos, compounds, new slang) and every unseen word becomes <UNK> — an information hole the model can never fill.

Single characters

Tiny vocabulary, nothing unknown — but sequences become 5× longer, which is quadratically more expensive in attention, and each token carries almost no meaning.

Subword tokenization takes the middle: frequent words become one token, rare words split into meaningful pieces, and anything else falls back to bytes so nothing is ever unrepresentable.

How BPE learns its vocabulary

Byte-pair encoding is trained, not designed:

  1. Start with every byte as its own token.
  2. Count adjacent pairs across a large corpus; merge the most frequent pair into a new token.
  3. Repeat until you reach the target vocabulary size (e.g. 100,000 merges).

Frequent sequences like the, ing and => become single tokens; rare words decompose. WordPiece (BERT) picks merges by likelihood gain rather than raw frequency; Unigram (SentencePiece) starts large and prunes; all three behave similarly in practice.

🌍
Tokenization is a fairness and cost issue

A tokenizer trained mostly on English gives ~4 characters per token for English but 1–2 for Hindi, Thai or Amharic. The same sentence therefore costs 2–4× more tokens, fits worse in the context window, and is more expensive to serve — for the languages least likely to be prioritised. When you pick or train a tokenizer, measure its fertility (tokens per word) on every language you support.

From token id to vector

The embedding table is a matrix of shape (vocab × d). Token id 15,231 means “take row 15,231”. Those rows are learned parameters, and for a 100k vocabulary with d=4096 they account for ~410M parameters at each end of the model — which is why the input and output embeddings are often tied (shared).

ids   = tokenizer("hello world")      # [15496, 1917]
x     = embedding_table[ids]          # (2, 4096)
h     = transformer(x)                # (2, 4096) contextual representations
logits = h @ embedding_table.T        # tied weights: (2, vocab)
📐
Static vs contextual embeddings

The embedding table gives one fixed vector per token — “bank” is the same vector everywhere. After a few transformer blocks, the representation is contextual: the “bank” in river bank and in bank account have diverged. Modern embedding models are transformers whose contextual outputs are pooled into one vector per sentence or document.

Embedding geometry

Meaning becomes direction. Similar items point the same way, and the standard score is cosine similarity (Chapter 2). Click a word to see its neighbours in this toy space.

Real embedding spaces have 384–4,096 dimensions, and nearly all pairs are close to orthogonal — high-dimensional space is mostly empty, which is exactly what makes nearest-neighbour search meaningful (Chapter 20).

How embedding models are trained

Contrastive learning: pull matching pairs together, push everything else apart. The pairs come from naturally aligned data — a question and its answer, a title and its body, a query and the clicked document, an image and its caption.

🧲

In-batch negatives

Every other item in the batch is a negative. Free, and bigger batches mean more negatives — which is why embedding training loves large batches.

🎯

Hard negatives

Mine almost correct documents with a first-pass retriever and train against those. This is usually the single biggest quality jump.

🪆

Matryoshka

Train so the first 64/128/256 dimensions are independently useful. You can then truncate vectors to cut storage by 8× and re-rank with the full vector.

🔢
Pooling and normalization matter

Mean-pooling token vectors, using the [CLS] position, or using the last token all give different results — use whatever the model card specifies, or your similarities will be quietly wrong. Then L2-normalize: with unit vectors, dot product equals cosine similarity, which makes vector databases faster and results consistent.

The systems view: storage and re-embedding

Embeddings are data you must store, index, back up and eventually regenerate. Size the bill before you choose a dimension.

Pin the embedding model version

Vectors from two model versions are not comparable — not even slightly. Upgrading the embedder means re-embedding the entire corpus and rebuilding the index, usually behind a dual-write and a shadow index so search never breaks. Store the model name and version alongside every vector, and treat an upgrade as a migration with a rollback plan (Chapter 21).

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Comparing vectors from different models or versions. The numbers still come out, and they mean nothing.
  • Forgetting to normalize when the index assumes cosine, or normalizing twice and wondering why scores are flat.
  • Using the wrong pooling for the model — a silent 5–15 point retrieval regression.
  • Counting characters instead of tokens for cost and context budgeting. Non-English and code blow the estimate apart.
  • Chunking without regard to tokens. “500 words” is 700 tokens in English and 2,000 in Japanese; chunk by tokens with overlap (Chapter 21).
  • Asking a model to manipulate letters and concluding it can't reason. It can't see letters — that's a tokenization artefact.

Interview drill

Warm-up: Why do LLMs fail at counting letters in a word?

How a strong answer sounds
The model never sees characters — it sees token ids. “strawberry” might be two or three tokens, none of which expose their spelling, so the model has to recall spelling as memorised knowledge rather than inspect it. It's a representation limitation, not a reasoning one, which is why the same model can write a correct program to count the letters. Workarounds: ask for a tool call or code, or use character-level or digit-split tokenization where it matters.

Deeper: You're choosing an embedding dimension for 200M chunks. Walk through the decision.

How a strong answer sounds
Start from retrieval quality on my data, not a leaderboard: build a small labelled query set and measure recall@k for a few models and dimensions. Then cost it: 200M × 1024 dims × 4 bytes is ~820 GB before the index, versus ~205 GB at int8 or ~100 GB at 384 dims — which decides whether it fits in RAM, and RAM is the dominant cost of a vector index. I'd prefer a Matryoshka-capable model so I can store truncated vectors for the first-pass search and re-rank the top few hundred with full vectors, plus scalar quantization. Finally, I'd pin the model version and plan the re-embedding migration before launch, because that cost recurs every upgrade.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Subword tokenization balances vocabulary size against sequence length; byte fallback means nothing is unrepresentable.
  • Token counts — not characters — drive context limits and cost, and they differ hugely across languages and code.
  • Embeddings turn meaning into direction; normalize and use the model's prescribed pooling.
  • Embedding models are trained contrastively; hard negatives usually give the biggest gains.
  • Vectors are infrastructure: budget storage, and treat an embedder upgrade as a full re-index migration.

Check your understanding

Why do most LLMs use subword tokenization rather than words?

A fixed vocabulary plus byte fallback means no <UNK> ever, at a sequence length far shorter than character-level.

The same paragraph costs 180 tokens in English and 520 in Thai. Why?

BPE merges reflect the training corpus. Under-represented scripts stay near byte level — more tokens, more cost, less usable context.

You upgrade your embedding model to a new version. What must happen?

Different models produce different spaces. Mixing them makes similarity meaningless — a full re-index, usually behind a shadow index with dual writes.

What are “hard negatives” in contrastive embedding training?

Random negatives are trivially separable and teach little. Near-misses mined with a first-pass retriever force the model to learn the fine distinctions that matter at retrieval time.

You store 50M embeddings at 1536 dimensions in float32. Roughly how much raw vector storage is that?

50e6 × 1536 × 4 bytes ≈ 307 GB, before the index overhead. This arithmetic is why int8 quantization and dimension truncation are standard practice.
