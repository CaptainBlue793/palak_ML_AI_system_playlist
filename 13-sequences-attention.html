Sequences & Attention

Text, audio, clickstreams and sensor traces all share one problem: the meaning depends on order and on things that happened far away. Recurrence was the first answer. Attention was the better one — and understanding why is the shortest path to understanding transformers.

  • Describe what LSTM gates fix and what they don't
  • Derive attention as “look up the relevant parts, weighted by similarity”
  • Read query, key and value roles and compute a weighted sum

Why sequences are different

A tabular row is self-contained. A sequence is not: “the trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big” requires linking a pronoun to a noun five words back. Three properties define the problem:

📏

Variable length

Sentences, sessions and audio clips differ wildly in length. Fixed-size inputs don't fit.

🔀

Order matters

“Dog bites man” ≠ “man bites dog”. Bag-of-words throws the difference away.

🔭

Long-range dependencies

The relevant context may be 5 tokens back, or 5,000. Both need to work.

Recurrent networks: carry a state forward

An RNN processes one element at a time, maintaining a hidden state that summarises everything so far: hₜ = tanh(Wₓxₜ + Wₕhₜ₋₁ + b). The same weights are reused at every step — parameter sharing across time, exactly as convolution shares across space.

An RNN, unrolledStep-through
  1. The first token is embedded and combined with an initial hidden state (usually zeros). Output: h₁, a fixed-size vector meant to summarise “the”.
  2. h₁ is fed in alongside the next embedding. Note the sequential dependency: step 2 cannot start until step 1 finishes. On a GPU this is the fatal flaw — no parallelism across time during training.
  3. Each step overwrites the state with a new mixture. Information about “trophy” must survive being re-mixed ten times to still be there when “it” arrives.
  4. To resolve “it”, the model needs the subject from ten steps ago — through a fixed-size bottleneck that has been multiplied by the same matrix ten times.
  5. Gradients flow backwards through every step, multiplying by the same weight matrix each time. Slightly-less-than-one factors vanish exponentially; slightly-more-than-one factors explode. This is the limitation of plain RNNs.
🧠
LSTM and GRU: gates that protect memory

An LSTM adds a cell state with additive updates and learned gates (forget, input, output) controlling what is kept, written and read. The additive path is the key: like a residual connection, it gives gradients a route with derivative ≈1, so information survives hundreds of steps instead of dozens. A GRU is a lighter two-gate variant with similar performance. Both extended the useful range dramatically — and neither fixed the sequential-training problem.

The encoder–decoder bottleneck

Early neural translation ran an encoder RNN over the source sentence, took its final hidden state, and decoded a target sentence from it. That single vector had to carry an entire sentence. Quality collapsed as sentences got longer — the model was being asked to memorise a paragraph in 512 numbers.

💡
The idea that fixed it

Instead of compressing the source into one vector, keep all the encoder states and let each decoding step choose which source positions to look at. That choice is a set of weights summing to one — and computing them from similarity is attention. Bahdanau's 2014 paper introduced it for translation; “Attention Is All You Need” (2017) then removed the recurrence entirely.

Attention from first principles

Think of a soft dictionary lookup. Each position offers a key (what I am) and a value (what I contribute). The position doing the looking has a query (what I need). Score the query against every key, softmax the scores into weights, and return the weighted average of values.

🔍 Query (Q)What this token is looking for. Derived from the current position: q = Wq·x.
🏷️ Key (K)What each token advertises about itself: k = Wk·x. The score is q·k.
📦 Value (V)What is actually retrieved and mixed: v = Wv·x. The output is Σ weightᵢ · vᵢ.
scores  = Q @ K.T / sqrt(d)      # similarity, scaled so softmax doesn't saturate
weights = softmax(scores)         # each row sums to 1
out     = weights @ V             # weighted average of values

Weights are illustrative, but the mechanism is exact: a softmax over dot products, producing a distribution over positions. Real models learn dozens of these patterns in parallel — one head tracks syntax, another coreference, another position (Chapter 14).

➗
Why divide by √d?

The dot product of two random d-dimensional vectors has standard deviation proportional to √d. Without scaling, scores for d=128 are large enough that softmax saturates into a near-one-hot distribution, gradients vanish, and training stalls. Dividing by √d keeps the score variance around 1 — a one-line fix for a real failure mode.

Self-attention vs cross-attention, and masking

Self-attention

Q, K and V all come from the same sequence: every token looks at every other token in its own context. This is what builds contextual representations.

Cross-attention

Q comes from one sequence, K and V from another: a decoder attending to an encoder, or a language model attending to retrieved documents or image patches.

Rows are queries, columns are keys. A filled cell means “this query may attend to this key”. Masking is implemented by setting blocked scores to −∞ before the softmax, so their weight becomes exactly zero.

Cost and parallelism: the actual trade

Attention is not cheaper than recurrence — it's quadratic in sequence length where an RNN is linear. It won because of parallelism: every position can be computed simultaneously during training, which maps perfectly onto a GPU, while an RNN must walk the sequence one step at a time.

More total arithmetic, but it all happens at once. A GPU with 10,000 lanes would rather do 10× the work in one parallel sweep than 1× the work in 1,000 dependent steps. (At inference, autoregressive decoding is sequential again — which is why Chapter 26 exists.)

📈
The quadratic wall

Doubling context quadruples attention cost and memory. That's why long-context work is a research area of its own: FlashAttention (same maths, far fewer memory round-trips), sliding-window and sparse patterns, linear-attention approximations, and state-space models like Mamba that revive a recurrent formulation with a parallel training algorithm.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting the causal mask in a generative model. It trains beautifully and generates nonsense — because it was reading the answer.
  • Not masking padding. The model attends to pad tokens, and batch composition silently changes results.
  • Omitting the √d scaling. Softmax saturates, gradients vanish, and the model appears to be “too small”.
  • Treating attention weights as explanations. They show where information was mixed from, not why the output was produced — a well-studied caveat.
  • Assuming longer context is free. Cost and memory are quadratic, and retrieval is often cheaper and more accurate than stuffing everything in (Chapter 21).
🎤
Interview drill

Warm-up: Attention costs O(T²) and an RNN costs O(T). Why did attention win?

How a strong answer sounds
Because the relevant currency is wall-clock time on parallel hardware, not FLOPs. An RNN has O(T) sequential steps — each depends on the last — so a GPU's thousands of lanes sit idle. Attention has O(1) sequential depth: all positions are computed in one batched matmul. It also gives every position a direct path to every other, so the gradient between distant tokens doesn't have to survive T multiplications. More arithmetic, radically better utilization and optimization.

Deeper: You need to model 100,000-token documents. Options?

How a strong answer sounds
First ask whether the whole document must be in context: retrieval over chunks is usually cheaper and often more accurate, since attention over 100k tokens dilutes as much as it helps. If full context is genuinely required: use FlashAttention to make the memory cost linear even though compute stays quadratic; use sparse or sliding-window attention with a few global tokens; consider a state-space or linear-attention hybrid; and exploit KV-cache reuse and prompt caching if the document is queried repeatedly. I'd also measure quality as a function of position — many long-context models degrade in the middle, so the engineering answer has to be validated, not assumed.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • RNNs carry a fixed-size hidden state; gradients through time vanish or explode, and training can't parallelise.
  • LSTM/GRU gates add an additive memory path that extends useful range, but keep the sequential bottleneck.
  • Attention is a soft lookup: score queries against keys, softmax, average the values.
  • Scaling by √d keeps softmax out of saturation; masks enforce causality and ignore padding.
  • Attention costs more FLOPs but has O(1) sequential depth — that parallelism is why it replaced recurrence.

Check your understanding

What is the main reason plain RNNs struggle with long-range dependencies?

Backpropagation through time multiplies the same Jacobian repeatedly; the product shrinks or grows exponentially with distance. Gates and additive paths were invented to work around exactly this.

In attention, what do the values (V) represent?

Keys decide how much attention each position gets; values are what is actually retrieved and averaged.

Why are attention scores divided by √d?

Dot-product magnitude grows with √d. Without scaling the softmax becomes nearly one-hot, its gradient goes to zero, and training stalls.

A GPT-style model must not attend to future tokens. How is that enforced?

−∞ becomes exactly zero weight after softmax. Zeroing values instead would still let the softmax denominator include them, subtly leaking information.

Doubling sequence length from 2k to 4k affects self-attention compute how?

The score matrix is T×T, so cost scales with T². This quadratic growth is what drives FlashAttention, sparse patterns and retrieval-based alternatives.
