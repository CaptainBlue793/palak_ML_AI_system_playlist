Making Models Cheap & Fast

The same answer, for a fifth of the money. Quantization, distillation, pruning and speculative decoding each attack a different part of the bill — and each trades away something you must measure rather than assume.

  • Quantize weights and activations, and know what breaks
  • Distil a large model into a small one that's good enough
  • Explain speculative decoding and estimate its speedup
  • Use cascades and routing so most traffic never touches the big model
  • Build a regression suite that catches compression damage

Quantization

Store weights in fewer bits. A tensor is divided into groups; each group gets a scale (and maybe a zero point) so its values map onto the available levels. Fewer bits means fewer levels, and the error shows up wherever the distribution has long tails.

Outliers are the whole problem

Transformer activations contain a few channels with values 10–100× larger than the rest. A single scale per tensor then has to cover that range, and every ordinary weight collapses into a couple of levels. Every good quantization method is an answer to this: smaller groups, per-channel scales, keeping outlier channels in high precision (LLM.int8), smoothing the scale between activations and weights (SmoothQuant), or using calibration data to minimise output error layer by layer (GPTQ, AWQ).

Distillation

Train a small student to imitate a large teacher. Crucially, the student learns from the teacher's full output distribution — its “soft labels” carry far more information than a hard answer, including how the teacher ranked the alternatives it rejected.

🗣️

Response distillation

Generate teacher outputs for your real traffic, then fine-tune the student on them. Simple, effective, and the standard approach for narrowing a general model to your task.

📊

Logit / KL distillation

Match the teacher's full next-token distribution with a KL loss. More sample-efficient, but needs access to logits — so usually only for models you host.

🧠

Reasoning distillation

Have the teacher produce step-by-step traces, keep only the ones that reach verified-correct answers, and train the student on those. Surprisingly strong on maths and code.

🎯
Distil on your own traffic, not a benchmark

The student only needs to be good at what your users actually ask. Sample real production prompts (with permission and PII handling), generate teacher answers, filter them with a verifier or a judge, and train. A 3B student trained on 50k of your real queries routinely matches a 70B model on that distribution — and is no substitute for it anywhere else. Keep the big model as a fallback for the tail.

Pruning and sparsity

Remove weights that contribute least. The catch is that hardware doesn't speed up for randomly scattered zeros — you save memory but not time.

Unstructured

Zero out the smallest weights anywhere. High sparsity is achievable with little quality loss, but you need specialised kernels to see any speedup, and usually you don't.

Structured (2:4, heads, layers)

Remove whole heads, layers or a fixed pattern (2 of every 4 weights) that tensor cores support natively. Real 1.5–2× gains, larger quality cost, and usually a recovery fine-tune afterwards.

Speculative decoding

Decode is sequential and memory-bound, so the GPU is idle most of the step. A small draft model proposes k tokens cheaply; the big model then verifies all k in a single forward pass — the same cost as producing one token. Accepted tokens are kept; the first rejection resets. The output distribution is provably identical to the target model's.

Cascades and routing: the biggest lever

Most production traffic is easy. Running every request through your largest model is usually the most expensive design decision in the system.

1. cheap classifier / heuristic  → is this simple?
2. small model answers            → confidence or verifier check
3. if unsure, escalate to big     → the expensive path, now rare

The escalation signal is the hard part

Routing only works if you can tell, cheaply and before answering, that a request is hard — or tell afterwards that the cheap answer is unreliable. Options: a small trained classifier on the prompt, the small model's own confidence (weak but usable), a verifier (unit tests, a schema check, a retrieval-support check), or a judge model on a sample. Measure the escalation rate and the quality of the non-escalated answers, or you're silently shipping the cheap model's mistakes.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Accepting a vendor's “no quality loss”. It means no loss on their benchmark; run yours.
  • Quantizing everything uniformly. Embedding, first/last layers and outlier channels usually need higher precision.
  • Expecting speedups from unstructured sparsity. Without matching kernels, scattered zeros save memory and no time.
  • Distilling only on benchmark data. The student ends up good at the benchmark and weak on your traffic.
  • Using speculative decoding at very large batch sizes. It helps most when you're memory-bound; at high batch the GPU is already busy and the draft is overhead.
  • Compressing before measuring. Establish the quality baseline and the cost baseline first, or you can't tell whether the trade was good.

Interview drill

Warm-up: Why does int4 weight quantization speed up single-user decoding so much?

How a strong answer sounds
Because decoding at batch 1 is memory-bandwidth bound: every step reads all the weights from HBM to produce one token, so time is essentially bytes-moved divided by bandwidth. Quartering the bytes roughly quarters that time. It does not help much at large batch, where the weight read is amortised across many sequences and the kernel is closer to compute-bound — which is exactly why quantization is a bigger win for interactive single-stream latency than for bulk throughput.

Deeper: Your serving bill is $180k/month for a 70B model. Where do you start?

How a strong answer sounds
Measure first: the split between prefill and decode tokens, the distribution of request difficulty, and the prefix-cache hit rate. Then in order of expected payoff: (1) routing — if 60–70% of traffic is simple, a distilled small model plus escalation cuts the bill more than any kernel work; (2) prompt-caching and context trimming, which reduce prefill directly; (3) fp8 or int8 weights and a quantized KV cache, which raise concurrency per GPU; (4) speculative decoding for the interactive path; (5) batching and scheduling tuning. Each step ships behind a quality guardrail with a task-specific eval and a canary, and I'd track cost per successful request rather than cost per token — the metric that rewards not calling the big model at all.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Match the technique to the bottleneck: bandwidth (quantization), parameters (distillation), sequential latency (speculation), volume (routing).
  • Quantization quality is decided by outlier handling and group size, not just bit width.
  • Distillation on your own traffic gives large models' quality on your distribution — and nowhere else.
  • Speculative decoding is free quality: identical outputs, 1.5–3× faster when memory-bound.
  • Cascades usually beat every micro-optimisation; the hard part is a trustworthy escalation signal.

Check your understanding

Why does speculative decoding not change output quality?

The acceptance test is constructed so the resulting samples come from exactly the target model's distribution. The draft only proposes; the target decides.

What makes transformer weights hard to quantize to very low bit widths?

One shared scale must cover the outliers, crushing everything else into a couple of levels. Per-group scales, outlier isolation and calibration-based methods all attack this.

You prune 60% of weights unstructured and see no speedup. Why?

Memory falls if stored sparsely, but the matmul is unchanged. Structured patterns like 2:4, which tensor cores accelerate natively, are what produce real speedups.

Which change most reduces cost when 70% of your traffic is simple FAQ-style questions?

Not running the big model at all beats making it cheaper. Quantization multiplies savings on the remaining 30%.

After quantizing to int4, which evaluation is most likely to reveal a problem that chat benchmarks miss?

Precision loss compounds over long dependency chains. Casual chat quality can look unchanged while maths, code and long-context retrieval degrade measurably.
