Privacy, Governance & Responsible AI

A model trained on personal data is a new copy of that data in a form you cannot grep. Fairness metrics you'd assume are compatible are provably not. And a growing body of regulation now asks for documentation you can only produce if you started collecting it a year ago.

  • Handle personal data through the ML lifecycle, including deletion
  • Explain differential privacy and federated learning, and their costs
  • Measure fairness — and know why you can't satisfy every definition at once
  • Produce the documentation regulators and auditors ask for
  • Run a governance process that doesn't paralyse delivery

🗑️
“Delete my data” and the model

You can delete rows from a warehouse in seconds. Removing a person's influence from trained weights is another matter: exact unlearning generally means retraining. The workable position, which you should decide and document before the request arrives: delete from all stores and pipelines immediately; exclude from all future training; record the model versions that were trained on the data; retrain on a defined cadence so the exposure has a known lifetime. Deduplication and privacy-preserving training reduce how much any single person's data affects the weights in the first place.

Memorisation is measurable

Large models can reproduce training data verbatim — more so for text that appeared many times or is highly distinctive (keys, addresses, rare names). Two practical controls:

🔁 Deduplicate aggressively

Duplication is the strongest predictor of memorisation. Near-duplicate removal (Chapter 16) reduces both compute waste and privacy exposure — the same operation solves two problems.

🐤 Plant canaries

Insert unique synthetic secrets at known frequencies, then try to extract them after training. This turns “could it memorise?” into a number you can track across model versions and report.

Differential privacy

DP gives a mathematical guarantee: the output is nearly the same whether or not any single individual was in the dataset. In training, DP-SGD clips per-example gradients and adds calibrated noise. The privacy budget ε quantifies the guarantee — smaller is stronger — and it is paid for in accuracy.

Federated learning is a complementary tool: raw data never leaves the device, only updates do. It is not private by itself — updates leak information — so it is usually combined with secure aggregation and DP.

Fairness, and why you can't have it all

Three reasonable-sounding definitions, all of which someone will ask for:

⚖️

Demographic parity

Equal positive rates across groups. “Approve the same share of each group.”

🎯

Equalised odds

Equal true-positive and false-positive rates. “Equally accurate for everyone.”

📏

Calibration by group

A score of 0.7 means 70% for every group. “The number means the same thing.”

🧮
The impossibility result

When base rates genuinely differ between groups, you cannot satisfy calibration and equalised odds simultaneously (except in degenerate cases). This is arithmetic, not a modelling failure — the same result that drove the COMPAS recidivism debate, where both sides were correctly citing different, mutually incompatible definitions. The engineering consequence: you must choose a fairness criterion, justify it in terms of the harm you're preventing, document the choice, and measure it continuously.

Explanations have an audience

A regulator wants documented process and evidence. A support agent wants a one-line reason they can say out loud. An affected customer wants to know what to change. An engineer wants to debug. These are four different artefacts, and shipping a SHAP plot to a customer satisfies none of them.

Regulation you'll actually meet

ProhibitedSocial scoring by public authorities, manipulative systems exploiting vulnerabilities, and certain biometric categorisation. Not a compliance exercise — you don't build these.
High riskEmployment, credit, education, essential services, critical infrastructure, law enforcement. Requires risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, human oversight, accuracy and robustness evidence, and conformity assessment. This is where most enterprise ML lands.
TransparencyChatbots must disclose they're AI; synthetic media must be labelled; deepfakes disclosed. Cheap to comply with if designed in, awkward to retrofit.
MinimalSpam filters, recommendations, most internal tooling. Voluntary codes of practice — but sector rules (financial model risk management, medical device regulation, employment law) may still apply.

Sector regulation usually bites before AI-specific regulation does: model risk management in banking, medical device rules for clinical tools, employment law for hiring tools. Ask “what regulates this decision?” before asking “what regulates AI?”.

⚙️
Make governance a pipeline stage, not a committee

Most of this checklist is producible automatically from artefacts you already have: the model card from the registry (Chapter 31), slice metrics from evaluation, lineage from the pipeline, monitoring configuration from the deployment. Teams that generate governance evidence as a build artefact ship weekly and pass audits; teams that write documents by hand before each release do neither.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Dropping the protected attribute and declaring fairness. Proxies remain; you've only lost the ability to measure.
  • Promising a fairness metric without checking compatibility with the others you also promised.
  • Logging full prompts with personal data by default. The logs become the largest privacy liability in the system.
  • No deletion story for models. The request will arrive, and “we can't” is not an answer you want to give first.
  • Governance as a launch-day document. If the evidence isn't generated by the pipeline, it will be invented from memory.
  • Assuming a vendor's model absolves you. You remain the deployer, and the obligations attach to the decision you make.
🎤
Interview drill

Warm-up: A user exercises their right to erasure. What happens to your model?

How a strong answer sounds
Immediately: delete from all stores, caches, feature stores, logs and backups per the documented process, and exclude the records from all future training sets — which requires that training sets are built from a filtered view rather than a frozen copy nobody can trace. For models already trained, exact unlearning generally means retraining, so the practical position — decided and documented in advance — is to record which model versions used the data, retrain on a defined cadence so exposure has a known lifetime, and rely on deduplication and privacy-preserving training to limit any individual's influence in the first place. If the data was genuinely high-risk, retraining sooner is the honest answer. What you must not do is discover this position during the request.

Deeper: Your credit model is calibrated but has different false-positive rates across groups. Legal asks you to fix it. What do you say?

How a strong answer sounds
That both properties can't hold when base rates differ — it's an arithmetic impossibility, not a bug — so the organisation has to choose which harm it is minimising and record that choice with its justification. I'd quantify the trade concretely: show approval rates, error rates and calibration by group under each option, and translate them into people affected. Equalising error rates means the score no longer means the same thing across groups, which has its own legal implications; keeping calibration means accepting the error-rate gap and mitigating the harm elsewhere — human review for borderline cases in the disadvantaged group, better data for that population, or a different decision policy. I'd also check whether the gap is driven by a feature acting as a proxy, and whether the label itself is biased, because that's a data problem masquerading as a fairness trade-off. Then document the decision, the evidence and the monitoring in the model card.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Personal data obligations follow through training and serving; decide the model-deletion position in advance.
  • Deduplication and canaries make memorisation measurable; DP and federated learning trade accuracy for guarantees.
  • Fairness definitions are mutually incompatible when base rates differ — choose, justify, document, monitor.
  • Removing the protected attribute hides the problem rather than fixing it.
  • Generate governance evidence from the pipeline so compliance and delivery speed aren't opposed.

Check your understanding

You remove the protected attribute from the feature set. What have you achieved?

Postcode, device, purchase history and name all correlate with protected attributes. You need the attribute to measure outcomes, even if the model doesn't use it as an input.

Base rates differ between two groups. Which pair of fairness criteria is impossible to satisfy simultaneously?

A formal impossibility result: with unequal base rates you can have calibrated scores or equal error rates, not both. The choice must be made and justified explicitly.

What does a smaller ε mean in differential privacy?

ε bounds how much any single individual can influence the output. Tighter bound, more noise, lower utility — usually recovered by training on more data.

Which factor most increases the chance a model memorises a specific training string?

Duplication is the strongest known predictor. Near-duplicate removal reduces both compute waste and privacy exposure at once.

Under risk-tiered AI regulation, which use is most likely “high risk”?

Employment decisions affect people's access to livelihood, so they carry documentation, oversight and robustness obligations. Ask what regulates the decision, not the technology.
