Training Deep Networks in Practice

Backprop is the theory. This is the craft: the initialization, normalization, optimizer, schedule and regularization choices that decide whether a model converges in two hours or diverges into NaN at step 300.

Initialization: the first thing that can kill a run

Each layer multiplies its input by a weight matrix. If those weights are slightly too small, activations shrink layer after layer until the signal is noise; slightly too big and they explode. The fix is to scale the random initialization by the layer's fan-in.

Normalization: keep the scale under control

Initialization sets the scale at step 0. Normalization layers keep it stable for the rest of training, by re-centring and re-scaling activations inside the network.

📦

BatchNorm

Normalizes each feature across the batch. Great for CNNs. Depends on batch size, behaves differently at inference (running averages), and is awkward for sequences and distributed training.

📏

LayerNorm

Normalizes across features within one example. Batch-size independent, identical at train and inference — the default for transformers and anything sequential.

⚡

RMSNorm

LayerNorm without the mean subtraction: just divide by the root-mean-square. Slightly cheaper, works as well — used in most recent LLMs.

🏗️
Pre-norm vs post-norm

Original transformers normalized after each sub-layer (post-norm) and needed careful warmup to avoid diverging. Modern ones normalize before (pre-norm: x + f(norm(x))), which keeps a clean residual path and trains far more stably at depth. If you ever see a training run that only works with a long warmup, post-norm is a likely culprit.

Optimizers: SGD, momentum, Adam

Plain SGD steps directly against the gradient. Momentum accumulates velocity so it powers through noise and small ravines. Adam additionally keeps a per-parameter running estimate of gradient magnitude and divides by it, giving every parameter its own effective learning rate.

Adam costs memory, and that memory decides your cluster

Adam keeps two extra floats per parameter (first and second moments). For a 7B model in mixed precision that's roughly 14 GB of weights + 14 GB of gradients + 56 GB of optimizer state in fp32 — the optimizer is the biggest single item. This arithmetic is why ZeRO and FSDP exist (Chapter 23).

Learning-rate schedules

A constant learning rate is almost never optimal. You want to start small (weights are random and gradients are wild), rise to a peak, then decay so the model can settle into a minimum.

🔥
Why warmup exists

At step 0, Adam's second-moment estimate is based on almost no data, so its effective step size is erratic and can be enormous. Ramping the learning rate over the first few hundred to few thousand steps lets those statistics stabilise. For large-batch and transformer training, removing warmup is one of the most reliable ways to blow up a run.

Precision, clipping and accumulation

Three practical mechanics that appear in every real training script.

🎚️ Mixed precision

Compute in bf16/fp16, keep a fp32 master copy of weights. Roughly 2× faster and half the activation memory. bf16 has fp32's exponent range so it doesn't need loss scaling — prefer it where hardware supports it.

✂️ Gradient clipping

Rescale gradients whose global norm exceeds a threshold (commonly 1.0). Turns a would-be divergence into a slightly wasted step. Effectively mandatory for language models.

➕ Gradient accumulation

Sum gradients over k micro-batches before stepping to simulate a k× larger batch on small GPUs. Remember to divide the loss by k, or your effective learning rate silently multiplies.

The debugging order

When a run misbehaves, work down this list rather than randomly changing hyperparameters.

Overfit 16 examplesCan the model drive the loss to ~0 on a tiny batch? If not, it's a bug — labels, masking, a frozen layer, a detached graph — not a tuning issue.
Check the input pipelinePrint one batch. Shapes, dtypes, value ranges, and the label next to the input. Eyeball five samples decoded back to text/image.
Watch gradient norms per layerVanishing (≈1e-8) or exploding (≈1e4) points straight at initialization, normalization or learning rate.
Sanity-check the loss at step 0For K-class cross-entropy it should be ≈ln(K) (e.g. 2.30 for 10 classes, ~10.8 for a 50k vocabulary). A wildly different value means a label or shape bug.
Run the LR range testIncrease the learning rate exponentially over a few hundred steps and plot the loss. Take roughly one-tenth of the rate where it first diverges.
Only then tuneSchedule, weight decay, dropout, batch size. Change one thing at a time and log it (Chapter 31).
🎲
Reproducibility is partial, and that's fine

Seed Python, NumPy and the framework, and log the seed. Bit-exact reproducibility additionally needs deterministic kernels (often slower), fixed data order and identical hardware and library versions. For most work, aim for “same conclusions across seeds” instead — and if a result only appears with one seed, it isn't a result. Report mean ± std over 3 seeds for anything you'd defend in a review.

Hyperparameter search that isn't a waste of GPUs

Random > grid

With 2 important and 6 irrelevant parameters, a grid wastes almost all of its budget on the irrelevant axes. Random sampling explores the important ones far better for the same cost. Sample learning rate and weight decay log-uniformly.

Kill bad runs early

Successive halving / ASHA: start many short runs, keep the best fraction, extend them, repeat. Combined with Bayesian search (Optuna, Ray Tune) this is typically 5–10× cheaper than running everything to completion.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Applying weight decay to biases and LayerNorm parameters. It quietly hurts; exclude them (most reference implementations do).
  • Dropout left on at inference. Frameworks handle this with train/eval modes — forgetting model.eval() produces mysteriously noisy predictions.
  • Changing batch size without touching the learning rate. Effective step size changes; scale the rate accordingly and keep warmup.
  • Not dividing by the accumulation count. Gradient accumulation without normalization multiplies your effective learning rate by k.
  • Tuning on the test set. Every hyperparameter decision belongs to validation.
  • Chasing hyperparameters when the data is the problem. A 2% tuning gain will not rescue a leaked feature or noisy labels.

Interview drill

Warm-up: Your transformer diverges at step 400 with loss going to NaN. Name five things you'd check, in order.

How a strong answer sounds
(1) Learning rate and warmup — is the peak rate reached too early for this batch size? (2) Gradient clipping — is it on, and what's the global norm just before the spike? (3) Precision — fp16 without loss scaling overflows; switch to bf16 or enable the scaler. (4) Data — is there a corrupt batch at that step? Log the batch index and re-run it alone. (5) Initialization and normalization — pre-norm vs post-norm, and residual scaling with depth. I'd also save a checkpoint just before the spike so I can replay the exact batch rather than guessing.

Deeper: Why does AdamW exist when Adam already supports weight decay?

How a strong answer sounds
In Adam, L2 regularization is added to the gradient, so it then gets divided by the same per-parameter second-moment estimate as everything else. Parameters with large historical gradients therefore receive much weaker decay — the regularization strength becomes an accident of gradient history. AdamW decouples it: the decay is applied directly to the weights, independent of the adaptive scaling. In practice this makes weight decay tunable in a meaningful way and generalizes better, which is why it is the default for transformer training.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Initialize with fan-in scaling (He for ReLU, Xavier for tanh) so activation scale survives depth.
  • LayerNorm/RMSNorm for sequences, BatchNorm for CNNs; pre-norm residual blocks train more stably.
  • AdamW is the default for transformers; its optimizer state dominates training memory.
  • Use warmup plus decay, and find the peak rate with an LR range test rather than folklore.
  • Debug in order: overfit 16 examples, inspect the batch, check gradient norms, verify step-0 loss — then tune.

Check your understanding

You're training a 24-layer transformer. Which normalization is the natural default?

LayerNorm normalizes within an example, so it's independent of batch size and identical at train and inference — essential for variable-length sequences and distributed training.

A 10-class classifier's loss at step 0 is 6.8 instead of about 2.3. What does that suggest?

A randomly initialized model should be uniformly uncertain: ln(10) ≈ 2.30. A much higher value means the outputs are confidently wrong — often shuffled labels or a shape mismatch.

You double the batch size from 256 to 512. What should you usually do to the learning rate?

A bigger batch gives a less noisy gradient estimate, so you can afford a larger step. Linear scaling with warmup is the standard recipe; √2 scaling is a more conservative alternative.

What problem does gradient clipping solve?

A single bad batch can produce a gradient orders of magnitude larger than usual. Clipping caps the global norm, turning a potential divergence into a slightly wasted step.

Why is bf16 usually preferred over fp16 for training on modern hardware?

bf16 trades mantissa precision for dynamic range. Deep learning tolerates low precision but not overflow, so bf16 removes a whole class of training instability — with no loss-scaling machinery.
