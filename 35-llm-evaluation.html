Evaluating LLM Systems

With a classifier you compute accuracy. With a generative system there is no single right answer, humans are slow and expensive, benchmarks are contaminated, and the model you use as a judge has opinions of its own. Evaluation is the hardest engineering problem in applied LLM work — and the one that decides whether you can ship confidently.

  • Build a golden set from real traffic that actually discriminates
  • Pick the cheapest grader that measures what you care about
  • Use LLM judges without inheriting their biases
  • Size an eval set and know when a difference is real
  • Run regression evals in CI and keep them honest over time

Three layers of evaluation

🏆

Capability benchmarks

MMLU, GSM8K, HumanEval, arena rankings. Useful for choosing a base model, almost useless for your task — and frequently contaminated (Chapter 16).

🎯

Task evals

Your golden set, your rubric, your prompts. This is the number you make decisions with, and nobody can build it for you.

📡

Production evaluation

Continuous sampled grading of live traffic, plus implicit signals: thumbs, escalations, retries, edits. The only measurement of the system users actually meet.

📉
A public benchmark score is not a product decision

Two models three points apart on a leaderboard routinely swap places on a specific task, and contamination makes headline numbers unreliable in the direction that flatters. Use benchmarks to shortlist; use your own evals to choose. If you have no task eval, you are shipping on vibes — and vibes are systematically biased toward whatever you looked at most recently.

🐛
Every bug report becomes an eval case

This single habit compounds faster than any tooling. A user complains, you reproduce it, you add the case with the expected behaviour — and it can never silently return. After six months you have an eval set shaped exactly like your product's real failure modes, which no benchmark can give you.

Choosing a grader

✅ ProgrammaticExact match, regex, JSON-schema validity, unit tests, SQL execution, retrieval-support check. Free, deterministic, unambiguous. Use wherever possible — more tasks admit it than people assume.
📏 Reference-basedOverlap with a gold answer (ROUGE/BLEU), or embedding similarity. Cheap, weak: correlates poorly with quality for open-ended text.
🧑‍⚖️ LLM judge (rubric)Score against explicit criteria with reasons. Cheap, fast, scales — and biased in known ways you must control for.
⚖️ LLM judge (pairwise)“Which response is better, A or B?” More reliable than absolute scoring, and directly gives a win rate against the incumbent.
👥 HumanThe ground truth for subjective quality. Slow, expensive, and inconsistent unless you invest in guidelines and measure agreement (Chapter 3).
📈 Implicit production signalsThumbs, copies, edits, escalations, retries, session abandonment. Free and abundant; biased and lagging, but excellent as an alarm.
You are grading a customer-support answer against the retrieved sources. Score each dimension 0–2 and give one sentence of justification: grounded every factual claim is supported by a cited source complete addresses all parts of the customer's question actionable the customer can do something with it concise no padding, no restating the question safe no promises about refunds/policy beyond the sources Then output JSON only: {"grounded":n,"complete":n,"actionable":n,"concise":n,"safe":n,"why":"..."} Grade the ANSWER against the SOURCES only. Do not use outside knowledge. Ignore writing style, length and formatting unless a criterion mentions them.

Specific criteria with a fixed output schema beat “rate this 1–10”. The explicit instruction to ignore length is there because judges reward verbosity unless told not to.

The four rules for LLM judges
  • Swap and average. Grade (A,B) and (B,A); if the verdict flips, it's a tie. This removes position bias almost entirely.
  • Calibrate against humans. Have people grade 100–200 of the same items and report agreement. A judge you haven't validated is a random number generator with good prose.
  • Prefer pairwise to absolute. “Better than the incumbent?” is far more stable than “score out of 10”, and it's the decision you actually need.
  • Don't let a model grade its own family unchecked. Self-preference is real; use a different model as judge, or validate the bias explicitly.

Eval-driven development

Write the eval before the change. It forces you to state what “better” means, turns a prompt tweak into a measurable experiment, and prevents the common cycle of improving one case while silently breaking three. Teams that do this ship prompt and model changes weekly with confidence; teams that don't accumulate a system nobody dares to touch.

Keeping evals honest

RiskSymptomMitigation
SaturationEverything scores 97%; changes are invisibleRetire solved items, add harder ones from production failures

ContaminationSuspiciously strong results on public setsKeep your set private; check n-gram overlap
Judge driftScores move when the judge model is updatedPin the judge version; re-baseline deliberately and note it
Distribution driftThe eval no longer looks like production trafficResample from recent traffic quarterly
Grading the wrong thingHigh scores, unhappy usersCorrelate eval scores against real outcomes — if they don't move together, the eval is wrong
⚠️
Common mistakes
  • No task eval at all. Then every change is a debate about anecdotes.
  • Using an unvalidated LLM judge. Measure agreement with humans before you trust a single number from it.
  • Ignoring position bias. Always swap A/B and average, or your win rates are partly about ordering.
  • Evaluating only happy paths. Include unanswerable, adversarial and multilingual cases, or they break in production.
  • Comparing on different eval sets. Run both systems on identical items and compare per-item.
  • Treating a 2-point move on 100 items as real. That's inside the noise — report a confidence interval.
  • Publishing your eval set. It then becomes training data and stops measuring anything.
🎤
Interview drill

Warm-up: How would you evaluate a customer-support assistant?

How a strong answer sounds
Layered, cheapest first. Programmatic where possible: did it cite a source that exists, did it produce valid structured output, did it stay within policy limits, did it refuse when the sources don't contain the answer. Then a rubric-based LLM judge on groundedness, completeness and actionability, validated against 150 human-graded items so I know the agreement rate. Then pairwise win rate against the current production version on a golden set sampled from real tickets, including unanswerable and adversarial ones. In production: sampled human review weekly, plus implicit signals — escalation rate, thumbs-down, reopened tickets — as the alarm. The offline suite gates releases; the production signals tell me whether the offline suite is still measuring the right thing.

Deeper: Your judge says the new prompt wins 61% of comparisons. Do you ship?

How a strong answer sounds
Not yet. First, is 61% outside noise for the sample size? On 200 items the 95% interval is roughly ±7 points, so it's probably real but not comfortably. Second, is it position-bias-free — were comparisons run in both orders and averaged? Third, how well does this judge agree with humans on this task; if agreement is 70%, a 61% win rate is a much weaker claim than it sounds. Fourth, check the losses, not the wins: 39% of cases got worse, and if those cluster in one capability or language that's a regression I'd have to fix regardless of the average. Then check guardrails — length, cost, latency, refusal rate — and if all of that holds, ship behind a canary and confirm with production signals rather than treating the offline number as the decision.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Benchmarks shortlist models; your own task eval makes the decision.
  • Build the golden set from real traffic, keep it private, and feed every bug report into it.
  • Prefer programmatic graders; use pairwise LLM judges with order swapping and human calibration.
  • Report confidence intervals — small eval sets cannot see small differences.
  • Run fast evals in CI, full suites nightly, and sampled human review on production continuously.

Check your understanding

Your LLM judge rates response A higher when it is shown first. What's the standard fix?

Position bias is well documented. Order swapping removes most of it and costs only a second judging pass.

Which grader should you prefer when the task allows it?

Deterministic, free, unambiguous and instant. Far more tasks admit a programmatic check than teams initially assume.

On a 100-item eval, version B scores 3 points higher. What can you conclude?

The 95% interval at n=100 is roughly ±9 points. Use a paired per-item comparison and a bootstrap, or a larger set.

Why should a golden eval set stay private?

Contamination is why headline benchmark numbers drift upward without matching real-world gains. Your private set is the only one you can trust over time.

Your eval scores keep improving but users are no happier. What's the most likely problem?

Correlate eval scores against real outcomes periodically. If they don't move together, the eval is the thing that needs fixing — and hold back a rotating slice you never iterate on.
