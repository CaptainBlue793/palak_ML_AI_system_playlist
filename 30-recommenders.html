Recommenders & Ranking Systems

Pick 20 items out of 200 million, personalised, in 100 milliseconds, and do it in a way that doesn't quietly turn your product into a monoculture. Recommenders are the most commercially valuable ML systems in existence, and the ones where the feedback loops bite hardest.

  • Design the multi-stage retrieval → ranking → re-ranking funnel
  • Build candidate generators, including two-tower retrieval
  • Handle implicit feedback, position bias and negative sampling
  • Balance relevance against diversity, freshness and business rules
  • Recognise feedback loops and design exploration to escape them

Why a funnel

You cannot score 200 million items with a heavy model in 100 ms. So you use a cascade: each stage handles fewer items with a more expensive model. Cheap and approximate at the top, expensive and precise at the bottom.

Retrieval sets the ceiling

The ranker can only order what retrieval hands it. If the perfect item isn't in the 1,000 candidates, no amount of ranking sophistication recovers it. Teams routinely spend months tuning the ranker while the retrieval stage silently drops the best item — so measure candidate recall (how often the eventually-engaged item was in the candidate set) as a first-class metric.

Two-tower, precisely

Train two encoders — one for the user/context, one for the item — so that a dot product approximates relevance, using in-batch negatives (Chapter 15). Item vectors are precomputed nightly and indexed; at request time you embed the user once and run an ANN query. The separation is the point: the item tower can be computed offline, which is what makes millisecond retrieval over hundreds of millions of items possible. The cost is that the two sides never interact before the dot product, so subtle feature crosses have to wait for the ranker.

Ranking

The ranker sees a few hundred candidates and can afford hundreds of features per item: user history, item statistics, context (time, device, session), and crosses between them. Modern rankers are usually multi-task.

# one model, several heads — trained jointly, combined at serving
p_click, p_watch_30s, p_like, p_share, p_hide = model(user, item, context)

score = w1·p_click + w2·p_watch_30s + w3·p_like + w4·p_share − w5·p_hide

# the weights encode product strategy, and they are argued about constantly.
# optimising p_click alone is how you get clickbait.
🎣
Your objective becomes your product

A feed that optimises clicks gets clickbait. One that optimises watch time gets longer, slower videos. One that optimises engagement gets outrage. This isn't a modelling failure — the system is doing exactly what you asked. Multi-task heads with explicitly negotiated weights, plus explicit negative signals (hide, report, “not interested”) and long-horizon metrics like next-week retention, are how teams keep the objective pointed at something they actually want.

Position bias and implicit feedback

Your training labels are clicks — and clicks depend enormously on where an item was shown. Naively training on them teaches the model that whatever it already ranked first is good.

Position bias and its correctionInteractive
📉

Corrections: inverse propensity weighting (divide each label by the estimated probability the item was examined at that position), a position feature set to a constant at serving time, or randomised position swaps on a small traffic slice to estimate the curve honestly.

BiasWhat it doesMitigation
Position biasTop slots get clicks regardless of qualityPropensity weighting, position feature, randomised swaps
Exposure biasYou only observe feedback on what you chose to showExploration traffic, logging what was not shown, off-policy correction
Popularity biasPopular items get more exposure, becoming more popularPopularity-debiased sampling, diversity constraints, calibration by segment
Selection biasOnly engaged users generate dataModel the missingness; evaluate on new and lapsed users separately

Negative sampling

You observe positives (clicks). Negatives must be constructed, and the choice materially changes the model: random negatives are too easy and teach little; in-batch negatives are convenient and skewed toward popular items; hard negatives — items shown but not clicked — are the most informative and the most confounded by position bias. Most production systems mix all three.

Cold start and exploration

🆕

New item

No interactions, so collaborative signals are empty. Use content embeddings, creator priors, and a deliberate exploration budget so it can earn data.

👤

New user

Popularity by region and device, an onboarding preference step, and fast adaptation from the first session's signals — the first ten minutes decide retention.

🎲

Exploration

A small share of slots allocated by a bandit (Thompson sampling / UCB) rather than the ranker. Without it, the system can only ever learn about what it already shows.

🌀
The feedback loop, stated precisely

The model chooses what is shown → users interact with what is shown → that interaction becomes tomorrow's training data → the model becomes more confident about its own choices. Left alone, this collapses diversity, entrenches popular items and makes the system blind to anything it stopped showing. The defences are structural: exploration traffic, logging impressions (not just clicks), off-policy evaluation, and diversity guardrails that are monitored as SLOs rather than tuned once.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Tuning the ranker while retrieval drops the answer. Measure candidate recall first.
  • Training on clicks without position correction. The model learns to reproduce yesterday's ranking.
  • Only logging clicks. Without impressions you cannot compute propensities, negatives or off-policy estimates.
  • Optimising a single engagement metric. It will be achieved, in the most literal and least desirable way.
  • No exploration. The system becomes confidently blind to everything outside its current repertoire.
  • Ignoring cold start. New items never accumulate data, so the catalogue silently shrinks to what was popular a year ago.

Interview drill

Warm-up: Why separate retrieval from ranking instead of using one model?

How a strong answer sounds
Compute. A ranker with hundreds of features and full user–item interaction costs milliseconds per item; you cannot run it over 200 million items in a 100 ms budget. Retrieval uses a factorised model whose item side can be precomputed and indexed for approximate nearest-neighbour search, giving sub-10 ms candidate generation at the cost of not modelling user–item interactions. The funnel lets each stage use the most expensive model it can afford for the number of items it sees — and the key metric linking them is candidate recall, because the ranker can only order what retrieval returns.

Deeper: Engagement is up 8% after a launch, but you're uneasy. What do you check?

How a strong answer sounds
Whether the gain is the kind you want. I'd break the 8% down by surface and user segment — is it concentrated in a few heavy users, or broad? Check the negative signals that don't appear in engagement: hides, reports, unfollows, “not interested”, and session-level satisfaction. Check health guardrails: catalogue coverage, creator concentration, share of impressions going to new items, topic diversity. Then look at longer horizons — next-week and next-month retention — because short-term engagement gains from more sensational content frequently reverse. And I'd check whether the metric moved for a mechanical reason, like more items per screen or an autoplay change, rather than better recommendations. If the long-horizon metrics are flat and the guardrails moved the wrong way, that 8% is a cost, not a win.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Use a funnel: cheap approximate retrieval, expensive precise ranking, constrained re-ranking.
  • Two-tower models make personalised retrieval over hundreds of millions of items feasible.
  • Implicit feedback is biased by position and exposure — correct it or you learn your own ranking.
  • Multi-task objectives with explicit weights keep the system pointed at the outcome you want.
  • Exploration, diversity guardrails and cold-start handling are what stop the feedback loop closing.

Check your understanding

Your ranker is excellent but engagement is flat. Candidate recall@1000 is 0.42. What do you fix?

Retrieval sets the ceiling. No ranking improvement can surface an item that wasn't retrieved.

Why can the item tower of a two-tower model be computed offline?

The factorisation is the whole trick: precompute and index item vectors, embed the user once at request time, and let ANN search do the rest. The price is no user–item feature interaction before the dot product.

Training a ranker on raw click logs without position correction leads to what?

Position dominates the click signal. More data makes the model more confident in the same self-confirming pattern — which is exactly why propensity correction and randomised swaps exist.

Which signal is most useful for escaping a recommender's feedback loop?

The loop is a data problem: you only learn about what you show. Only deliberate exploration generates information about the rest of the catalogue.

You optimise purely for click probability. What's the predictable outcome?

The system optimises exactly what you asked. Multi-task objectives with negative signals and long-horizon metrics are the standard defence.
