The ML System Design Interview Playbook

An ML design interview isn't a quiz about architectures. It's forty-five minutes to demonstrate that you can turn a vague business problem into a system with a metric, a data story, a serving budget and an honest account of what will break. Here's the structure that makes that legible.

  • Run a 45-minute session with a repeatable structure
  • Say the right things in each phase — and know what to skip
  • Recall the numbers that make estimates credible
  • Recognise what separates a mid-level answer from a staff-level one
  • Practise deliberately instead of re-reading

What's actually being assessed

🧭

Judgement

Do you pick the simplest thing that works, and can you say why the complicated thing isn't needed yet?

📊

Breadth

Data, features, modelling, serving, monitoring — can you cover the whole lifecycle without being led?

🔬

Depth

When pushed on one part, do you have real detail — or does it stop at the box you drew?

🗣️

Communication

Do you drive the session, check in, and adapt when challenged rather than defending?

Say this plan out loud in the first minute: “I'll spend five minutes on requirements, then metrics, data, features, a baseline and model, serving, and finish with monitoring and trade-offs — tell me if you'd rather go deeper somewhere.” It signals structure and lets the interviewer redirect you early, which is what they want.

🎯
The three sentences that improve most answers

“The baseline is X — the model has to beat that to be worth the complexity.”
“The labels arrive N days late, so my monitoring uses these leading indicators.”
“I'd choose A over B here, and I'd switch to B if [specific condition].”
Each one signals production experience rather than coursework, and most candidates say none of them.

What separates the levels

MidNames the right components and builds a sensible pipeline. Metrics are generic; serving is sketched; monitoring is mentioned once.
SeniorDerives the metric from the decision, does the arithmetic, budgets latency by stage, designs the label pipeline including delay, and names the failure modes before being asked.
Staff+Questions the framing (“is a model the right intervention here?”), states what they'd ship in week one versus quarter two, identifies the organisational risk — feedback loops, incentives, who owns the pager — and gives a decision rule for when the design should change.
🚩
Red flags interviewers score against
  • Jumping to a model before defining the prediction task and its metric
  • No baseline, so no way to know whether any of this is worth building
  • Ignoring serving entirely — a design that can't meet its latency budget
  • Claiming a technique without being able to explain its cost (“we'd use RLHF”)
  • Silence for long stretches, or a monologue with no check-ins
  • Defending a choice when given new information rather than updating
  • Inventing numbers with false confidence — say the assumption out loud instead

A two-week practice plan

Days 1–3: re-read Chapters 7, 26, 29, 34 — metrics, serving, skew, monitoring are where most answers are thin. Days 4–10: one case study per day from Level 5, out loud, on a whiteboard, 45 minutes each, then compare with the chapter. Days 11–13: use the mock interview tool with the timer and score yourself against the rubric; target your two weakest rubric rows. Day 14: rehearse the opening two minutes and the closing summary until they're automatic — they frame everything in between.

Interview drill

Opening: “Design a system to detect fake reviews.” What are your first 90 seconds?

How a strong answer sounds
“Before I design anything: what decision does this drive — do we remove the review, hide it pending review, or flag it for a human queue? That changes the precision/recall balance completely. What's the scale — reviews per day, and how many human reviewers do we have? What's the latency requirement: is this at submission time or a nightly sweep? And what's out of scope — am I also handling seller collusion and incentivised reviews, or just individual fake ones? My assumption unless you say otherwise: 2M reviews a day, a review queue with capacity for ~5,000 a day, decisions within a minute of submission, and the metric is recall at the precision our reviewers can sustain. Does that match what you had in mind?”

Closing: How do you spend the last three minutes?

How a strong answer sounds
Summarise the design in four sentences — task and metric, data and labels, model and serving, monitoring and rollout. Then volunteer the two weakest points: “the label pipeline depends on human review, which is itself biased toward what the current model surfaces, so I'd add randomised sampling into the review queue; and my latency budget assumes the feature store answers in under 5 ms, which I'd want to verify first.” Then say what you'd ship first: “week one, the heuristic rules plus logging, so we have the data to train on at all.” Naming your own weak points reads as senior, because it's what you'd do in a design review — and it directs the remaining questions somewhere you're prepared for.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • State your plan in the first minute and drive the session through eight phases.
  • Derive the metric from the decision, and always name the baseline you must beat.
  • Serving, delayed labels and monitoring are where most answers are thin — that's where to be strong.
  • Know the arithmetic well enough to estimate out loud without hedging.
  • Name your own weaknesses and give a decision rule for when the design should change.

Check your understanding

You're asked to “design a recommendation system”. What's the best first move?

Every later choice depends on what's being recommended, to whom, at what scale and in what budget. Synthesise the answers into stated assumptions rather than asking open-endedly for ten minutes.

Roughly how much compute does training a 13B model on 3T tokens need?

6 × 13e9 × 3e12 ≈ 2.3e23. At ~1e15 useful FLOP/s per GPU that's around 2,700 GPU-days — the kind of estimate you should be able to do out loud.

Which statement most signals production experience?

Label delay and the monitoring it forces is a problem you only meet by running systems. The others are recallable from a blog post.

The interviewer challenges your database choice. What's the strongest response?

Interviewers are testing whether you reason about conditions or recite preferences. Making the decision rule explicit is the answer that scores.

You have five minutes left and haven't covered monitoring. What do you do?

Breadth is scored. A design with no monitoring or rollout story reads as someone who has never operated a model — and the model section rarely earns more marks than the first mention of drift and retraining.
