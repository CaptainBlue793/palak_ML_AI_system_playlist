Evaluation, Metrics & Validation

Training is easy to get working and hard to get wrong quietly. Evaluation is the opposite: it looks trivial, and almost every serious ML failure traces back to measuring the wrong thing, on the wrong data, at the wrong threshold.

  • Pick a metric from the decision the model drives
  • Read confusion matrices, ROC and PR curves — and know when each lies
  • Set a threshold from costs, not from 0.5
  • Check calibration before feeding probabilities into money
  • Evaluate slices, quantify uncertainty, and run honest error analysis

Validation: the discipline of not fooling yourself

Three datasets, three jobs. Train fits parameters. Validation chooses everything else — hyperparameters, architectures, thresholds, whether to ship. Test gives one honest number, and every time you look at it and change something, it becomes a little more like a validation set.

📚

k-fold CV

Split into k parts, train k times, average. Uses all the data for both training and evaluation. Best when data is scarce and rows are independent.

📅

Rolling-origin CV

For time series: train on weeks 1–4, test week 5; train 1–5, test 6; and so on. The only honest CV when the future is what you predict.

👥

Grouped CV

Keep all rows of an entity (user, patient, seller) inside one fold, or you're measuring memorisation.

🎰
Multiple comparisons: how teams overfit the validation set

Try 200 model variants and pick the best validation score, and you've partly selected for luck — expect a drop on test. Mitigate by keeping a genuinely untouched test set, preferring simpler models when scores are within noise, and reporting a confidence interval rather than a point estimate.

Why ROC-AUC flatters imbalanced models

The false-positive rate divides by the huge negative count, so 5,000 false positives out of 980,000 legitimate transactions is an FPR of 0.5% — invisible on an ROC curve. Precision divides by the number of flags, so those same 5,000 next to 300 true catches mean precision of 6%: your review team drowns. With a rare positive class, report PR-AUC and precision at the recall you actually operate at.

Thresholds come from costs, not conventions

0.5 is the default only because it's the middle. The right threshold minimises expected cost:

flag if  P(positive) × cost_of_miss  >  (1 − P(positive)) × cost_of_false_alarm

# fraud: £500 miss, £8 false alarm  →  flag above p ≈ 0.016
# spam:  £0.05 miss, £5 false alarm (lost email) →  flag above p ≈ 0.99

In practice you often invert the question: “we can review 2,000 cases a day — what threshold gives us the best recall at that volume?” That's recall at fixed review capacity, and it's what operations teams actually care about.

Calibration: are the probabilities real?

A model is calibrated if, among all cases it scored 0.30, about 30% are positive. Ranking metrics don't care; any decision involving money, thresholds or downstream arithmetic cares enormously.

Fixes: Platt scaling (fit a logistic regression on validation scores), isotonic regression (non-parametric, needs more data), or temperature scaling for neural nets — divide logits by a single learned T. All are fitted on a held-out set, never on training data.

⚖️
Resampling breaks calibration

If you oversampled positives from 1% to 50% to help training, the model now believes the world is 50% positive and its outputs are wildly too high. Either undo the shift analytically (adjust the intercept by log(w)) or recalibrate on a held-out set with the true base rate. This bites people constantly in fraud and ads.

Averages hide everything interesting

A 4-point aggregate gain can contain a 20-point regression for new users. Slice your evaluation by every dimension that matters commercially or ethically, and set alerts on the worst slice — not the mean.

Is the difference real?

Model A scores 0.843, model B scores 0.847. Before you celebrate, ask whether that gap survives resampling. Two cheap tools:

🔁 Bootstrap CI

Resample your test set with replacement 1,000 times, recompute the metric each time, and report the 2.5th–97.5th percentiles. If the intervals overlap heavily, the models are tied.

🪙 Paired test

Compare models on the same examples and test the differences (McNemar for classification, paired bootstrap otherwise). Pairing removes example difficulty as a source of noise and needs far less data.

🔬
Error analysis beats one more metric

Take 100 errors, read them, and put each into a bucket. You will typically find that 40% are label noise, 25% are one systematic failure mode, and 35% are genuinely hard. That tells you exactly what to do next — relabel, add a feature, or accept the ceiling — which no aggregate number ever will.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reporting accuracy on imbalanced data. The majority-class baseline is already 99%; the metric carries no information.
  • Tuning the threshold on the test set. Thresholds are a hyperparameter — choose them on validation.
  • Comparing models at different thresholds. Compare curves, or compare at a matched operating point (same recall or same review volume).
  • Ignoring calibration when the score feeds a decision rule. A miscalibrated 0.9 and a calibrated 0.9 lead to different, expensive actions.
  • Evaluating only on the average. Slice by user tenure, locale, device and time, or the regression will find you in production.
  • Letting the test set rot. Reuse it fifty times and it is no longer a test set — refresh it periodically from new data.
🎤
Interview drill

Warm-up: Your model has 0.98 ROC-AUC on fraud detection. The ops team says it's useless. Explain how both can be true.

How a strong answer sounds
With a 0.2% base rate, ROC-AUC is dominated by the enormous negative class: a tiny false-positive rate still means thousands of alerts per day. At the threshold they operate, precision might be 4%, so reviewers see 24 legitimate cases for every real fraud and stop trusting the queue. The right framing is precision at their review capacity — “with 500 reviews a day, we catch X% of fraud at Y% precision” — and PR-AUC as the headline offline metric. Then tune the threshold on expected cost, not on 0.5.

Deeper: How would you decide whether a 0.4% offline improvement is worth shipping?

How a strong answer sounds
First, is it real? Paired bootstrap on the same test examples with a confidence interval; if it straddles zero, it's noise. Second, is it real where it matters? Check the slices and the operating point rather than the aggregate curve. Third, what does it cost? A 0.4% gain from a model that triples serving cost or adds 40 ms of latency is usually a loss. Finally, the offline number is only a prediction of the online one — so the decision is whether to spend an A/B slot on it, and the size of the expected effect determines how long that test must run to detect it.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Pick metrics from the decision and its costs; accuracy is almost never the right headline.
  • On imbalanced problems use PR-AUC and precision at an operable recall, not ROC-AUC.
  • Thresholds are chosen from expected cost or review capacity, on validation data.
  • Calibrate before any probability feeds money, and re-calibrate after resampling.
  • Slice everything, quantify uncertainty, and read 100 errors before tuning anything else.

Check your understanding

Disease affects 1% of patients. Your classifier reports 99% accuracy. What is the most useful next question?

99% is the always-negative baseline. Only the positive-class metrics reveal whether it detects anything at all.

You lower the decision threshold from 0.5 to 0.2. What happens?

A lower bar flags more cases, catching more true positives (higher recall) and more false ones (lower precision). The model is unchanged; only the operating point moves.

A model's scores rank perfectly but every probability is roughly double the true rate. Which metric is unaffected?

AUC depends only on the ordering, so any monotone distortion leaves it untouched. That's precisely why AUC alone cannot tell you the probabilities are safe to act on.

You oversampled positives from 1% to 50% during training. What must you do before using the probabilities for a money decision?

The model learned a world where positives are 50% likely, so its outputs are massively inflated. Adjust the intercept analytically or fit a calibration map on a held-out set with the real prevalence.

Model B beats model A by 0.003 AUC on a 5,000-row test set. What's the right response?

On 5,000 rows a 0.003 gap is well within noise. Pair the comparison on identical examples and look at the interval before spending an experiment slot.
