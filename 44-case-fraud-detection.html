Case Study: Real-Time Fraud Detection

Score every card transaction in 50 milliseconds, catch fraud that is 0.15% of volume, and do it against an adversary who is actively testing your defences and will change tactics the week after you deploy. The interesting constraints here are not modelling ones.

  • Turn fraud losses and false declines into a single decision rule
  • Build streaming velocity and graph features inside a 50 ms budget
  • Train with delayed, biased and adversarially-shifting labels
  • Design the human review loop as part of the system, not around it
  • Keep the system honest when the attacker adapts

🚦
The first design question: what happens if the model is down?

Fail open (approve everything) loses money to fraud for the duration. Fail closed (decline everything) stops all revenue and is far more damaging. The real answer is a fallback ladder: model → cached score for this card → a small rules engine → approve with a conservative amount cap. The decision has to be made in advance, written down, and rehearsed — and it's a business decision, not an engineering one.

🎯
Metrics

Primary (online): net fraud loss per £1,000 processed, plus the false-decline rate — the two must be reported together or one will be optimised into the other. Offline: PR-AUC and recall at the precision the review team can sustain. Guardrails: approval rate by segment, p99 latency, review queue depth, and appeal overturn rate.
Baseline: the existing rules engine. It will be strong on known patterns and useless on new ones — which is exactly the shape of the argument for adding a model.

😤
False declines cost more than fraud

Industry experience is consistent: legitimate customers wrongly declined cost multiples of the fraud prevented, because a declined customer often doesn't come back and tells other people. A model that reduces fraud 30% while raising false declines 1 percentage point is usually a net loss. This is why the threshold must come from expected cost (Chapter 7) rather than from a default — and why the false-decline rate belongs next to the loss number in every report.

🕸️
Graph features precomputed, refreshed near-line

“This device has been used with 14 cards in the last hour” is one of the strongest fraud signals in existence and one of the most expensive to compute. The pattern: maintain the entity graph in a streaming job, materialise per-entity aggregates (degree, distinct counts, component risk) into the online store, and read them as plain features. Latency budget met, signal preserved, and the graph job is where the interesting engineering lives.

3 · Defence in depth

RulesHard blocks for known-bad entities, sanctions lists and velocity ceilings. Instant to update — crucial during an active attack, when waiting for a retrain is not an option.
ModelGradient-boosted trees over the features above. Fast on CPU, handles mixed types and missing values natively, retrains in minutes (Chapter 6).
PolicyThreshold per amount band and per segment, derived from expected cost. Step-up authentication (3-D Secure, one-time code) as a middle option between approve and decline — often the highest-value action available.
Human reviewA bounded queue of the highest-value uncertain cases. Also your fastest source of labels and your window into new attack patterns.
Post-hocBatch detection of patterns visible only in aggregate — mule networks, testing sprees, merchant collusion — feeding back into rules and the graph.

5 · Deep dive: labels are late, biased and adversarial

⏳

Late

Chargebacks confirm at 30–90 days. Any performance metric computed today describes a model from three months ago (Chapter 34).

🎭

Biased

You only observe outcomes for transactions you approved. Declined transactions have no label — the classic selection-bias trap in fraud.

🥷

Adversarial

The distribution shifts because of your model. Yesterday's signal is today's thing the attacker avoids.

🔍
Recovering labels for what you declined

Three practical routes: (1) approve a small randomised sample of would-be declines below a value cap — an explicit, budgeted exploration cost that buys unbiased labels; (2) use human review outcomes as labels, remembering that reviewers see only what the model surfaced; (3) use downstream signals on approved-but-suspicious transactions (customer contact, refund requests, account takeover reports). Without at least one of these, your model's view of the world narrows to the shape of its own past decisions.

🧪
Attackers probe your thresholds

Card testing — thousands of tiny transactions to find which cards work and which amounts pass — is both an attack and a reconnaissance tool. Signals: a burst of small amounts across many cards from one device or IP range, unusually high decline rates from a merchant, sudden clustering just under a known threshold. Defences: velocity rules at the entity level, deliberately fuzzy thresholds so the boundary isn't exactly learnable, rate limiting per device and per merchant, and alerting on the decline distribution rather than only on approvals.

🎤
Interview drill

Follow-up: “Fraud losses dropped 40% after launch. Is that success?”

How a strong answer sounds
Not on its own — I'd want the false-decline number next to it. Declining more transactions always reduces fraud; the question is what it cost in legitimate customers, and that cost is larger per unit than the fraud saved. So: approval rate before and after, by segment and by amount band; the appeal overturn rate; and ideally a holdout group whose transactions were scored but not acted on, which gives an unbiased estimate of both sides. I'd also check where the reduction came from — if it's concentrated in one merchant or one geography, it may be a data artefact rather than better detection. And I'd look at the review queue: if it grew beyond capacity, the apparent gain may just be cases quietly timing out into a default decision.

Follow-up: “A new attack pattern appears on Saturday night. What happens?”

How a strong answer sounds
The model won't catch it — it has never seen the pattern and the labels are 90 days away. That's what the rules layer is for: an on-call analyst can deploy an entity-level velocity rule or a hard block within minutes, with a documented review and an expiry so it doesn't become permanent technical debt. In parallel, the case goes into the review queue with a priority flag, the graph job surfaces connected entities so the block covers the network rather than one card, and the labelled cases go into the next retrain. The design principle is that the fast layer is human-editable rules and the slow layer is the model — attempting to solve a Saturday-night attack by retraining is how people lose a weekend and a lot of money.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Report fraud loss and false declines together; the threshold comes from expected cost per amount band.
  • Velocity and graph features carry the signal — precompute graphs near-line to meet the latency budget.
  • Labels are late, biased by your own decisions and adversarially shifting; budget for randomised approvals.
  • Rules give you a minutes-fast response layer; the model gives you generalisation. You need both.
  • The review queue is part of the system: prioritise by expected value and harvest structured labels from it.

Check your understanding

Why is a single global decision threshold a poor choice here?

Expected-cost reasoning gives a different optimum per amount band: aggressive on £4,000, permissive on £8.

You only observe fraud outcomes for approved transactions. What problem does this create?

The model's blind spots are self-perpetuating because it never sees the outcome of what it blocked. A randomised approval sample below a value cap buys unbiased labels.

A new attack starts tonight. Which layer responds fastest?

Model retraining needs labels that are months away. Rules exist precisely to cover the window between an attack appearing and the model learning it.

Why precompute graph features rather than traversing the graph at request time?

Maintain the graph in a streaming job and materialise per-entity aggregates; the scoring path then reads plain features.

How should the review queue be prioritised?

Reviewer time is the scarce resource. A moderately suspicious large transaction is worth more attention than a highly suspicious tiny one.
