Monitoring, Drift & ML Observability

A broken web service pages you. A broken model returns 200 OK with a slightly worse answer, every time, for three months. ML monitoring exists because the failure mode is silence — and because the metric you most want to watch usually arrives weeks late.

  • Monitor four layers: system, data, model and business
  • Distinguish data drift, concept drift and label shift, and measure each
  • Watch quality without labels, using prediction and proxy signals
  • Design alerts people don't learn to ignore
  • Triage an ML incident to the right layer quickly

Four layers, four kinds of question

SystemIs it up and fast? Error rate, p50/p99 latency, saturation, queue depth, GPU memory, cost per request. Familiar, and necessary but nowhere near sufficient.
DataAre the inputs what we expect? Schema, null and default rates, feature distributions, freshness, volume. Most ML incidents start here.
ModelIs the behaviour stable? Prediction distribution, confidence, per-slice accuracy once labels arrive, calibration, refusal and fallback rates.
BusinessIs it still useful? Conversion, fraud losses, tickets deflected, escalation rate, complaints. Slow, noisy, and the only thing that actually matters.
🧾
Log one row per prediction

Almost all ML observability comes from a single well-designed log line. Without it, every investigation begins with instrumentation work you should have done months earlier.

{ request_id, timestamp, user_segment, model_name, model_version, features_hash, feature_values (or a sampled subset), defaults_used, prediction, confidence, threshold, decision, latency_ms, fallback_used, prompt_version, cache_hit, cost_estimate }

Sample heavily if volume or privacy demands it — 1% of requests fully logged is enough to detect drift, and far better than 100% of nothing. Labels join onto this table later by request_id.

Rules of thumb for PSI: <0.1 stable, 0.1–0.25 moderate shift worth investigating, >0.25 significant. Treat them as triggers for a look, not as automatic retraining decisions.

🔕
Drift is a symptom, not a verdict

Inputs can drift with no effect on accuracy, and accuracy can collapse with no visible input drift. Alert on drift to prompt an investigation, and reserve paging for things that map to user harm: the fallback rate, the null-rate spike, the business metric, the slice that broke. Teams that page on every PSI excursion stop reading the alerts within a month.

Monitoring quality when labels are late

Fraud labels take 60 days. Churn labels take 30. Loan defaults take years. You cannot wait, so you watch leading indicators.

📈

Prediction drift

The distribution of scores the model outputs. If the mean predicted fraud rate doubles overnight, something changed — inputs, population, or the model.

🧑‍⚖️

Proxy labels

Human review outcomes, early signals (a chargeback filed, a session abandoned), rules-based checks. Biased, but fast — and correlated enough to be an alarm.

🎯

Sampled ground truth

Deliberately label a small random sample continuously. Expensive per item and the only unbiased signal you'll have before the real labels land.

⏳
Backfill your dashboards

When labels finally arrive, recompute historical performance and write it back. You then get an honest curve of “how good was the model in week 12?” that's simply late — which is what tunes your leading indicators. Without backfilled evaluation you never learn whether your proxies were telling the truth.

Common mistakes
  • Monitoring only system metrics. Uptime is 100% while quality quietly halves.
  • No per-prediction log. Every investigation then starts with a week of instrumentation.
  • Paging on drift. Alert fatigue within a month, and then nobody reads the real alerts either.
  • Aggregate-only monitoring. A slice can collapse entirely while the average moves 0.3%.
  • No backfilled evaluation. You never learn whether your leading indicators were right.
  • Ignoring the null/default rate. The fastest signal of an upstream break, and the most often unmonitored.
🎤
Interview drill

Warm-up: How do you monitor a model whose labels arrive 60 days late?

How a strong answer sounds
Layered. Immediately: input data quality — schema, null and default rates, feature distributions against a training reference — plus the prediction distribution and the rate at which predictions cross the decision threshold. Same-day: proxies such as human-review agreement, early outcome signals and complaint rates. Continuously: a small randomly sampled set sent for labelling so I have an unbiased, if small, quality estimate. Then, as real labels land, backfill the historical evaluation and check whether the leading indicators moved when quality did — that calibration is what makes the early signals trustworthy rather than decorative.

Deeper: Prediction volume is normal, latency is normal, but conversion dropped 8% overnight. Walk through it.

How a strong answer sounds
First, is it the model at all? Check whether anything deployed — model version, prompt version, feature pipeline, upstream service, or a product change elsewhere in the funnel. Then the data layer: null and default rates per feature, and distributions versus reference; a single feature going constant is the classic cause and shows up instantly. Then the model layer: prediction distribution and threshold-crossing rate, by slice — an 8% aggregate drop is often one segment collapsing. Then serving: fallback rate and cache behaviour, since a silent fallback to a smaller model looks healthy on every system metric. If all of that is clean, it's probably not the ML system — traffic mix, a marketing campaign or an upstream page change can move conversion without anything in my stack changing. Throughout, I'd compare week-over-week at the same hour rather than to yesterday.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Monitor system, data, model and business layers — system metrics alone hide every ML failure.
  • Data drift is detectable without labels; concept drift is not, so use proxies and sampled ground truth.
  • Alert on user impact, ticket on drift, and give every alert a runbook.
  • Log one row per prediction and backfill evaluation when labels arrive.
  • Monitor slices: the average is the last thing to move.

Check your understanding

Fraudsters change tactics but your input distributions look unchanged. What kind of drift is this?

The same inputs now imply a different outcome. It is invisible to input monitoring, which is exactly why proxies and sampled labels matter.

Which signal most quickly reveals “an upstream pipeline broke and a feature is now constant”?

It shows up within minutes at the data layer. Accuracy will show it too — in sixty days.

PSI for a feature is 0.32. What's the appropriate response?

Drift is a symptom. It may be a harmless population change or the first sign of a break — investigate, but reserve paging for signals tied to user impact.

Why backfill evaluation metrics when delayed labels arrive?

Backfilled truth is how you calibrate the proxies you rely on day to day — otherwise they're guesses you never checked.

Aggregate accuracy is flat, but one customer segment has collapsed. What was missing?

A small segment can fail completely and barely move the mean. Track key slices individually and alert on each.
