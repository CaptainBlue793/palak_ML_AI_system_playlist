Case Study: Training a Frontier LLM

Ten thousand GPUs, ninety days, and a nine-figure budget spent on a single computation that either produces a frontier model or does not. There is no A/B test and no gradual rollout — you make the architectural decisions once, watch a loss curve for three months, and find out at the end. This chapter is the capstone: every earlier chapter shows up here, and the hardest problems turn out to be operational rather than mathematical.

  • Allocate a fixed compute budget between model size, data and inference cost
  • Build the data pipeline that decides the model's ceiling before training starts
  • Choose a parallelism strategy that fits memory and keeps the interconnect busy
  • Operate a 90-day run through hardware failures, stragglers and loss spikes
  • Decide, from evidence, whether the result is worth launching

🎲
What makes this different from every other chapter

Everywhere else in this course you ship, measure and iterate. Here the feedback loop is ninety days long and you get one attempt per budget cycle. The decisions that matter — model shape, data mixture, tokenizer, optimizer, learning-rate schedule — are made before a single full-scale step runs, and they are effectively irreversible by week two. The entire discipline is therefore about de-risking in advance: scaling-law fits from small runs, ablations at 1% scale, and instrumentation good enough that you notice a problem on day three rather than day forty.

💡
Compute-optimal is not deployment-optimal

Scaling laws tell you the model size that minimises training loss for a given training budget. That is the right answer only if you train once and never serve anything. If the model will answer a trillion tokens a month for two years, inference dominates total cost by a wide margin, and deliberately over-training a smaller model — spending more training compute than the compute-optimal point suggests, for a worse training loss than a bigger model would reach — produces a cheaper, faster product with a better total-cost picture. The compute-optimal point is a reference, not a target (Chapter 16).

3 · Data: the decision made before training starts

From raw crawl to training shardsStep-through
  1. Web crawl, books, code, papers, curated reference material, licensed corpora. Perhaps 100 trillion tokens raw, of which you will keep a seventh. Having surplus is what makes aggressive filtering affordable — quality is bought by throwing data away.
  2. Language identification, a learned quality classifier, heuristics for boilerplate and machine-generated spam, safety and CSAM removal, and PII scrubbing. Filtering is where most of the measurable quality comes from, and every filter is a value judgement about what the model will be good at (Chapter 16).
  3. Exact and fuzzy dedup across the whole corpus. Duplicated data wastes compute, encourages memorisation, and corrupts evaluation when a benchmark leaks in. Cross-checking against every eval set you intend to use is a mandatory step, not a nicety — and it is one of the most common ways frontier results turn out to be wrong.
  4. How much code, how much maths, how many languages, how much conversational text. Code improves reasoning even for non-code tasks; over-weighting one domain quietly costs another. Mixture weights are set from 1%-scale ablations, and a curriculum — general data first, high-quality and long-context material late — reliably beats a fixed blend.
  5. The tokenizer is frozen for the model's lifetime: vocabulary size trades embedding parameters against sequence length, and poor multilingual coverage becomes a permanent tax on every non-English user (Chapter 15). Shard so any worker can stream any shard, and make the ordering reproducible from a seed.
  6. Mixture weights, learning rate, batch schedule, architectural details — all chosen from small runs whose results extrapolate along a fitted scaling law. This is the single highest-value activity in the whole programme: a week of ablations routinely saves a month of full-scale compute, and nothing else recovers a wrong decision made on day zero.
  7. Checkpoint frequently, evaluate continuously on a fixed suite, and compare the loss curve against the predicted curve daily. A run that is 3% off prediction on day five is telling you something, and the cost of investigating is two days while the cost of ignoring it is eighty.

4 · Parallelism and the memory arithmetic

Parameters140B parameters in bf16 = 280 GB. Already far beyond one 80 GB GPU, before anything else exists.
Optimizer stateAdam keeps two moments plus an fp32 master copy: roughly 12 bytes per parameter, about 1.7 TB. This, not the weights, is what dominates training memory (Chapter 23).
GradientsAnother 280 GB in bf16, plus the reduction buffers for communication.
ActivationsScales with batch × sequence × layers. Selective recomputation trades roughly 30% extra compute for a large memory saving, and it is nearly always the right trade.
The planTensor parallel 8 inside a node over NVLink, pipeline parallel 12 across nodes, data parallel 107 groups on top with fully sharded optimizer state, plus sequence parallelism for long context. Roughly: fastest link for the chattiest dimension (Chapter 24).
The constraintEvery dimension buys memory with communication. Tensor parallelism beyond a node collapses on interconnect bandwidth; pipeline parallelism creates bubbles that interleaved schedules only partly hide; data parallelism needs a batch large enough to shard and small enough to still learn.

Sanity-check the plan with MFU before committing: MFU = achieved FLOPs ÷ peak FLOPs. Above 45% is good at this scale, below 30% means the interconnect or the pipeline schedule is wasting a third of a nine-figure budget — and that is worth a week of profiling before day one, not after.

5 · Eighty-eight days

Day 0Burn-in: a 6-hour full-scale run at the real configuration. Confirm MFU, checkpoint save and restore, the evaluation harness, and the dashboards. Teams that skip this discover a broken restore path on day nine and lose everything since the last verified checkpoint.
Day 1–3Loss falls fast and predictably. Compare against the scaling-law projection every few hours. Early divergence is the cheapest signal you will ever get — and the last point at which restarting is merely embarrassing rather than catastrophic.
Day 4First hardware failure. At 10,240 GPUs a node dies every few hours: memory errors, thermal events, a failed optical link. The run must detect, evict and resume from the last checkpoint automatically, or engineers spend ninety days restarting jobs by hand.
Day 11A straggler. One node runs 8% slow — a throttling GPU or a degraded link — and because every step synchronises, the whole cluster runs 8% slow. Per-rank step-time monitoring finds it; without that instrumentation it hides for weeks and costs millions.
Day 23A loss spike. Rewind to a checkpoint before it, skip the offending data batches, and resume — sometimes with a lower learning rate. Spikes correlate with pathological data, so the batches are worth inspecting: it is often a repeated document or an encoding artefact that dedup missed.
Day 40Evaluations plateau on one capability while loss keeps falling. Usually a mixture problem, and it is the most painful moment of the run: you can adjust the remaining curriculum, but you cannot un-train what has happened. This is exactly the decision the 1%-scale ablations existed to prevent.
Day 66A silent data corruption. A shard serves malformed tokens and loss degrades without any error being raised. Checksums on shards and an automatic sanity-check on batch statistics are the only defence; hardware does not always tell you it is wrong.
Day 88Learning rate decays to its floor; final checkpoints written. Pretraining is finished, and the model is not yet a product — post-training is a separate programme with its own risks (Chapters 17 and 18).
🔁
Determinism, checkpointing and the ability to rewind

A 90-day run will be interrupted, so the ability to resume exactly is a hard requirement rather than an operational nicety. That means checkpointing model, optimizer state, data-loader position and RNG state together, atomically; writing asynchronously so the cluster is not idle during a multi-terabyte save; and keeping enough history to rewind past a spike that is only recognised hours later. Teams that keep only the last two checkpoints discover on day 23 that both are already contaminated (Chapter 25).

6 · Evaluation and the launch decision

StageWhat it answersFailure mode it catches
Loss vs projectionIs the run healthy?Divergence, stragglers, data corruption — daily, during the run
Capability suiteReasoning, code, maths, multilingual, long-contextMixture problems, capability plateaus
Contamination auditDid benchmark data leak into training?The single most common reason a headline result is not real
Human preferenceIs it actually better to use?Benchmark gains that no user can feel
Safety and red-teamWhat can it be made to do?Harms that no capability benchmark measures (Chapter 36)
Serving economicsCan we afford to run it?A model that is excellent and unshippable (Chapters 26, 38)

The launch decision is not "did the loss go down". It is whether the capability gain is large enough to justify the serving cost, whether the safety evaluation is acceptable, whether the contamination audit is clean, and whether people prefer it in blind comparison against the model you already ship. A model can win on every benchmark and still be the wrong thing to launch.

🎤
Interview drill

Follow-up: “Day 30 of 88. Loss is 4% above the projected curve. What do you do?”

How a strong answer sounds
First, establish whether it is the model or the measurement. I would check the evaluation harness and the loss-computation path before believing anything, because a tokenizer or masking change in the eval code produces exactly this signature and costs nothing to rule out. If the gap is real, I would decompose it: is it uniform across data domains, or concentrated in one — which would point at the mixture or a corrupted shard? Are batch statistics normal, are shard checksums clean, did anything change in the data loader? Is throughput and MFU normal, since a straggler shows up as slow rather than worse, and the two get confused. Is it gradient-norm or learning-rate related — a schedule bug, or the aftermath of a spike that was partially recovered? Then the decision, which is genuinely hard: 4% at day 30 with a smooth curve and normal internals is often something you ride out, because rewinding costs a third of the budget and the projection itself has error bars fitted from small-scale runs. A 4% gap that is widening, or that coincides with a specific domain, is a rewind to the last clean checkpoint. What makes this answerable at all is having decided the thresholds in advance — before the run, when nobody has sunk £50M into the current trajectory and everyone's judgement is still clear.

Follow-up: “The model beats your current production model on every benchmark. Do you launch it?”

How a strong answer sounds
Not on that evidence alone. I would want four more things. A contamination audit — benchmark gains are the expected symptom of eval data leaking into a 14-trillion-token corpus, and this is the most common reason a frontier result turns out not to be real. Human preference in blind pairwise comparison on our actual traffic distribution, because benchmark gains regularly fail to show up in anything a user can feel. Safety and red-team results, since a more capable model is also more capable of harm and that evaluation is not optional. And serving economics: if it is 5% better and three times the cost per token, the right move may be to distil it, or to route only hard queries to it, rather than to replace the production model wholesale. Then I would ship it the way anything else ships — shadow, canary, staged rollout with guardrails on latency, cost and safety, and a rollback that someone has actually tested. The training run being finished does not make the model a product; it makes it a candidate.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Compute-optimal minimises training loss; if you are going to serve the model, deliberately over-training a smaller one usually wins on total cost.
  • Data filtering, dedup and mixture decide the model's ceiling, and they are fixed before the first full-scale step runs.
  • Ablations and scaling-law fits at 1% scale are the highest-return activity in the programme — nothing else recovers a wrong day-zero decision.
  • At 10,000 GPUs, failures, stragglers and silent corruption are routine; automatic resume, per-rank monitoring and a checkpoint ladder are load-bearing.
  • MFU is a financial metric at this scale — ten percentage points is millions of pounds.
  • The launch decision weighs capability, contamination, human preference, safety and serving cost together; a benchmark sweep decides nothing on its own.

Check your understanding

Why might you deliberately train a smaller model past its compute-optimal point?

Compute-optimal answers "lowest training loss for this training budget". If you serve a trillion tokens a month for two years, that is the wrong question.

What dominates memory during training?

140B parameters is 280 GB of bf16 weights but about 1.7 TB of optimizer state, which is why sharding it across data-parallel ranks is the first thing you do.

One node out of 10,240 runs 8% slower. What is the effect?

Synchronous data-parallel training moves at the pace of the slowest worker. Per-rank step-time monitoring is what turns an invisible multi-million-pound leak into a ten-minute eviction.

Why are ablations at 1% scale the highest-value activity in the programme?

A week of ablations routinely saves a month of full-scale compute. Nothing recovers a wrong mixture or learning-rate decision once the run is a fortnight in.

Your model wins on every public benchmark. What must be verified first?

Contamination produces exactly this symptom, and at 14 trillion tokens it is the default outcome unless you actively checked. Human preference, safety and serving cost are the other three gates.
