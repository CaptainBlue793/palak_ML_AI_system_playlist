Deployment, Rollout & Online Experiments

The offline metric is a prediction. The experiment is the measurement. Most of what separates teams that ship improvements from teams that ship changes is the discipline between those two sentences.

  • Roll out through shadow, canary and experiment with automated guardrails
  • Size an A/B test and know what effect it can actually detect
  • Avoid peeking, novelty effects and interference
  • Choose between A/B, interleaving and bandits
  • Read a result honestly and decide with it

The rollout ladder

1 · ShadowNew model runs on real traffic; outputs logged, never used. Catches skew, latency and crashes at zero risk (Chapter 32). Duration: days.
2 · Canary1–5% of traffic, watched by automatic guardrails on errors, latency, cost and a fast proxy metric. This is an operational safety check, not a measurement of value.
3 · ExperimentA proper A/B at a share large enough to detect the effect you care about, run for a pre-registered duration. This is where you learn whether it helps.
4 · RampIncrease share in steps, re-checking guardrails at each level. Some effects only appear under load or at scale.
5 · Full + holdbackShip to everyone, but keep a small long-term holdback to measure cumulative effects and catch slow regressions.
🛡️
Guardrails are not the same as the primary metric

The primary metric is what you hope will improve. Guardrails are what must not get worse, and they should trigger an automatic rollback without discussion.

If you must look early, use a method built for it

Sequential testing (always-valid p-values, mSPRT) and group-sequential designs with alpha spending let you monitor continuously without inflating error. CUPED — regressing out each user's pre-experiment behaviour — typically cuts variance by 30–50%, which shortens every test you run and is usually the single highest-leverage improvement to an experimentation platform.

A/B, interleaving, or bandits?

⚖️

A/B test

The default. Unbiased, interpretable, measures real product outcomes. Slow, and needs enough traffic for the effect size.

🔀

Interleaving

For ranking: blend two rankers' results in one list and see which side's items get clicked. 10–100× more sensitive because each user compares both — but it measures ranking preference, not product outcomes.

🎰

Bandits

Continuously shift traffic to what performs better. Great for many variants, short-lived decisions and minimising regret; harder to get a clean effect estimate and unsuitable for long-horizon metrics.

🧭
A practical sequence for ranking changes

Offline NDCG to shortlist candidates → interleaving to pick the winner cheaply and quickly → a full A/B on the winner to measure the product effect and check guardrails → ramp with a long-term holdback. Each stage is more expensive and more trustworthy than the last, which is exactly the property you want.

📏
Report the interval, not just the point

“+1.8%” invites a decision the data can't support. “+1.8%, CI +0.4% to +3.2%” says: probably positive, possibly small. Also state the cost of being wrong — a change that is free to reverse deserves a different bar than one that alters a pricing model or a safety threshold.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Peeking and stopping early. Turns a 5% error rate into 20–30%.
  • No sample-ratio mismatch check. A broken split invalidates everything downstream, silently.
  • Launching on a canary. A canary checks safety, not value — it is far too small to measure an effect.
  • Treating “not significant” as “no effect”. Report the interval; an underpowered test is uninformative, not negative.
  • Ignoring interference in marketplaces and social products, where treatment leaks into control.
  • No long-term holdback. Cumulative effects and slow regressions are invisible once everyone is treated.
🎤
Interview drill

Warm-up: Your test shows +3% conversion, p = 0.04, after six days of a planned fourteen. Ship it?

How a strong answer sounds
No — not on that basis. Stopping when the result first crosses significance is peeking, and it inflates the false-positive rate several-fold; effects caught early are also systematically overestimated. I'd run to the pre-registered horizon, or use a sequential design that is valid under continuous monitoring if early stopping matters commercially. I'd also check SRM, look for novelty by examining the daily effect, and confirm guardrails. If there's genuine urgency, the honest options are to switch to an always-valid method or to accept a documented, higher error rate — not to pretend the fixed-horizon p-value still means what it says.

Deeper: How would you A/B test a change to a two-sided marketplace's ranking?

How a strong answer sounds
Naive user-level randomisation is invalid because supply is shared: if treatment users book the best-rated providers, control users see worse availability, so the control group is contaminated and the effect is overstated. Options: randomise by market or region (cluster randomisation), accepting far fewer units and therefore much lower power; use switchback testing, alternating the whole market between variants in time blocks, which handles interference at the cost of needing careful block sizing versus carryover; or budget-split designs that partition supply. I'd also measure both sides — buyer conversion and provider utilisation and earnings — because a change that improves buyer metrics by concentrating demand on a few providers is usually bad for the marketplace. And I'd instrument the interference directly: check whether control-group availability moves when treatment share changes.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Shadow proves it runs, canary proves it's safe, an experiment proves it helps — they are not substitutes.
  • Size tests from baseline rate and effect size; halving the effect quadruples the sample.
  • Peeking inflates false positives — use a fixed horizon, or a sequential method designed for monitoring.
  • Watch for interference, novelty, SRM, ratio metrics and segment shopping.
  • Report confidence intervals and guardrails, and keep a long-term holdback.

Check your understanding

You check your A/B test daily and stop when p < 0.05. What's the real false-positive rate?

Each look is another chance for noise to cross the threshold. Fixed horizons or always-valid sequential methods are the fixes.

Your 50/50 experiment delivered 52.3% / 47.7% of users. What do you do?

At that scale the imbalance is far beyond chance. Something is systematically dropping or misassigning users, so the groups aren't comparable — fix it and re-run.

Why is interleaving so much more sensitive than an A/B test for ranking?

Between-user variance dominates A/B noise. Comparing within a user removes it — at the cost of only measuring ranking preference, not product outcomes.

A new recommendation model shows +12% engagement in week 1 and +1% by week 4. What is this?

Users react to change itself. Run long enough for the effect to stabilise and report the steady-state estimate, not the first week.

Which is a guardrail metric rather than a primary metric?

Guardrails must not get worse and should trigger automatic rollback. The primary metric is the one you're trying to move.
