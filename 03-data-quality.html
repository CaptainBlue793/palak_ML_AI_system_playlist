Data: Sourcing, Labeling & Quality

Models are a compression of their training data. Everything the data gets wrong — missing groups, noisy labels, accidental future information — the model will faithfully reproduce and then amplify. This is the chapter that saves you the most pain later.

  • Compare where training data comes from, and what each source costs you
  • Produce labels that are good enough, and measure how good they are
  • Split data so your offline number predicts online reality
  • Spot data leakage — the single most common cause of “too good” results
  • Handle class imbalance and validate data automatically

Where does training data come from?

There are five realistic sources, and most production systems use several. The question is never “which is best” but “what does each cost, and what does it bias?”.

Provenance is an engineering requirement, not paperwork

For every dataset, record where it came from, when, under what licence or consent, and who can be deleted from it. The day someone exercises a deletion right, or legal asks whether a corpus may be used commercially, “we scraped it in 2023” is not an answer. Chapter 37 covers the governance side; the engineering side is simply: version datasets and keep lineage.

Labels: the expensive part

A label is the answer you're teaching the model to produce. Getting them is usually the bottleneck, so people trade quality for volume in four standard ways.

👩‍⚖️

Expert labels

Radiologists, lawyers, senior agents. Highest quality, lowest volume, highest cost. Reserve for your evaluation set.

👥

Crowd labels

Many cheap annotators, 3–5 per item, majority vote. Needs clear guidelines and gold questions to catch bad workers.

🔖

Weak supervision

Heuristics, regexes and existing systems vote on labels; a model learns to reconcile them. Noisy but nearly free at scale.

🖱️

Implicit / natural

The world labels it for you: the transaction was charged back, the user re-ran the query, the ticket was reopened.

📏
Measure your labellers before you measure your model

Have two annotators label the same 300 items and compute agreement (Cohen's κ). If humans only agree 70% of the time, no model will exceed roughly 70% on that task — and your “model error analysis” will mostly be label disputes. Low agreement means the guidelines are ambiguous, not that the annotators are bad. Fix the definition first.

Splits: the contract with your future self

You split data into train (fit parameters), validation (choose hyperparameters and models) and test (one honest estimate, touched as rarely as possible). How you split matters more than the ratio.

⏱️
If time matters, split by time. Always.

Any system that predicts the future — churn, demand, fraud, recommendations — must be validated on data from after the training window. A random split lets the model learn from Tuesday to predict Monday, which it will never be able to do in production. The offline number will look wonderful and the launch will be a disappointment.

Leakage: why your model looks brilliant

Data leakage is any information in training that will not be available — or will not be honest — at prediction time. It is the single most common reason an offline result fails to reproduce online, and it is embarrassingly easy to introduce.

Spot the leakPractice0 / 7
🔍
The leak-detector heuristics
  • Suspiciously good. 0.999 AUC on a hard problem is a bug until proven otherwise.
  • One feature dominates. Check feature importance — if a single column carries the model, ask when that column is written relative to the label.
  • Ask “when is this known?” for every feature. Write the timestamp next to each one. Anything stamped after the prediction moment is out.
  • Preprocess inside the fold. Scalers, imputers, target encoders and vocabularies must be fitted on training data only, then applied to validation.

Imbalance: when the interesting class is 0.2%

Fraud, disease, defects, clicks on ads — the positive class is rare. Accuracy becomes meaningless (predict “no” and score 99.8%), and gradients are dominated by negatives.

⬆️ Resample

Oversample positives (or SMOTE), undersample negatives. Cheap, but changes the base rate — you must recalibrate probabilities afterwards (Ch 7).

⚖️ Reweight

Give rare-class examples a larger loss weight. Keeps all the data and usually beats resampling. Watch for overfitting to a handful of positives.

🎯 Change the metric

Often the real fix. Optimise and report PR-AUC, recall at a precision you can staff, or expected monetary loss — not accuracy.

Data quality, automated

You cannot eyeball a table that grows by 40 million rows a day. Treat data like code: define expectations, test them in the pipeline, and fail loudly.

CheckExample expectationAction on failure
SchemaColumn amount exists, is float, non-nullHard fail the pipeline — never train on it
Range / validity0 < age < 120, currency in enumQuarantine bad rows, alert, continue
VolumeRow count within ±25% of the 7-day medianAlert: upstream job probably failed
DistributionMean and null-rate within tolerance of last weekWarn — this is early drift detection (Ch 34)
Uniqueness / keysOne row per (user_id, event_id)Fail: duplicates silently reweight your training set
FreshnessMax event timestamp within 2 hoursAlert: stale features are worse than missing ones
🗂️
Version datasets like code

A model is only reproducible if the exact data is. Use immutable snapshots (a table version, a partition date, a content hash) and record that identifier next to every training run (Ch 31). “We trained on the users table” is not reproducible; “users@2026-04-02T00:00Z, sha 9f3c…” is.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Random-splitting time-dependent data. Instant leakage, wildly optimistic offline metrics, disappointing launch.
  • Fitting the scaler on all the data before splitting. The validation set's statistics have now leaked into training. Fit inside the fold.
  • Deduplicating after splitting. Near-duplicate rows land in both train and test, and your test set is partly a memory test. Dedupe first, split second.
  • Cleaning the test set. If you fix labels only where the model disagrees, you have optimised your evaluation, not your model. Fix systematically or not at all.
  • Not looking at 50 random rows. Ten minutes reading raw examples finds encoding bugs, truncated fields and mislabelled classes that no summary statistic surfaces.
🎤
Interview drill

Warm-up: Your churn model hits 0.97 AUC offline and does nothing in the A/B test. Walk through your debugging.

How a strong answer sounds
First suspect leakage: list every feature with the timestamp at which it is written, and look for anything derived from the cancellation event itself (support contacts about closing, a downgrade flag, the refund record). Second, check the split — if it was random over a time-ordered table, redo it temporally and see how much of the AUC survives. Third, check train/serving parity: are the online feature values computed the same way as offline? Only after those three would I look at the model. And separately: even a perfect predictor changes nothing if the intervention doesn't work — so I'd check whether the treatment, not the prediction, is the weak link.

Design: You need 100,000 labelled examples of “is this support reply helpful?” with a budget for 5,000 human labels. What do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
Spend the human budget on a clean, expert-labelled evaluation set plus enough to measure agreement (say 2,000 eval, 500 double-labelled for κ, 2,500 seed training). Generate the bulk via weak supervision — implicit signals like thread reopened, thumbs-down, escalation, customer reply sentiment — and reconcile those noisy sources into probabilistic labels. Then train on the weak labels and evaluate only on the clean set. If agreement on the human set is poor, stop and rewrite the guidelines: the task definition is the bug.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Every data source biases the model in a predictable way — name the bias when you pick the source.
  • Human agreement is your accuracy ceiling; measure it before blaming the model.
  • Split by time for anything predicting the future, and by group when rows share an entity.
  • Leakage is the default explanation for surprisingly good results — check when each feature becomes known.
  • Automate schema, volume, distribution and freshness checks; version datasets so runs are reproducible.

Check your understanding

You're predicting whether a loan will default. Which feature is leakage?

Collections calls only happen after payments are missed — the feature is a consequence of the label. At prediction time (application) it is always zero, so the model learns nothing usable.

Two annotators agree on only 65% of your toxicity labels. What should you do first?

65% agreement caps model performance and means the task definition is ambiguous. Dropping disputes biases the dataset toward easy cases; a bigger model cannot learn a rule humans can't state.

Your dataset has 1,000 images per patient from 200 patients. What split is correct?

Random splitting puts images of the same patient in both train and test, so the model can memorise the patient rather than the pathology. Group by the entity that repeats.

Fraud is 0.3% of transactions. Your model reports 99.7% accuracy. What has it most likely learned?

The majority-class baseline is 99.7%. Report PR-AUC or recall at an operable precision instead — and never let accuracy be the headline metric on imbalanced data.

Which pipeline step order avoids leakage?

Dedupe before splitting (so near-duplicates don't span splits), split by time, and fit every transform on training data only — then apply it to validation and test.
